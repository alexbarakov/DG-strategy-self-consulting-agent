# Domains and Data Mesh

## What the board teaches
"Domain structure is a key element in many DG practices going forward" — the board makes domain mapping the entry point into decentralized governance. Two complementary artifacts do the work: a registry-style Data Domain Classifier (domain / subdomain grid with Data Owner, Business Data Steward, and Tech Data Steward / Data Custodian columns — worked Finance and HR examples) and a visual hexagon domain map (six business domains with color-matched subdomain stickies and ~10 dotted cross-domain connectors, e.g. Sales-Operations via Client Master Data). From there the board steps into data-mesh territory: the Data Mesh Canvas (datamesh-architecture.com) classifies data products into source-aligned / aggregate / consumer-aligned swim lanes, with a worked example of 11 data products and 15 consumption dependencies (CRM's "360° Customer" aggregating source products; Controlling's "Profitability Reporting" drawing on both layers). Data Products themselves are defined as prepared, trusted, non-raw data ready for wide consumption — with concrete requirements (described/interpretable, guaranteed quality with transparent issues, change notifications, purpose-driven design, discoverability, interoperability) and the pragmatic advice that a simple spreadsheet register of critical sources is a perfectly good start.

## Key objects
- Takeaway: domain structure underlies modern DG practices (criticality labeling, stewardship, catalogs, mesh)
- Data Domain Classifier grid: Data Domain | Subdomain | Data Owner | Business Data Steward | Tech Data Steward / Data Custodian; examples — Finance (P&L, cost allocation, planning & forecasting) and HR (salary, attrition, trainings, headcount)
- Hexagon domain map (filled): Sales, Finance, Operations, Marketing, Human Resources, Services Delivery + subdomains; cross-domain connectors (Client Master Data shared Sales-Operations); peripheral placeholder hexagons; matching blank template
- Data Mesh Canvas layers: source-aligned (Search Queries, Articles, Orders, Customers, Shipment, Invoice) → aggregate (ML Model, 360° Customer) → consumer-aligned (Funnel Analytics, KPIs & Dashboard, Profitability Reporting)
- Data Product definition and 6 requirements: labeled/described data, guaranteed quality with issue transparency, change notifications, purpose-driven with captured consumer requirements, accessibility/discoverability, interoperability (connect/auth/download)
- "Simple register could be a good start": spreadsheet register of critical sources / data models with Google Sheets template
- Domain classifier, domain map, and mesh canvas are all workshop templates — see [templates.md](../12_templates/templates.md)

## From the course (Data Governance Fundamentals, 6 days)

### The definition that does the work
- The author's own formulation, dictated in the session rather than taken from a book: a data domain is "a delineated area of data tied to a specific business function, unit or line of activity in the company, having a **unique set of responsible people**." The last clause is the whole definition — the rest is decoration. (course day 3, transcript; slides p.12)
- Why the abstraction is needed at all: "it is not obvious to many people that data — a set of objects of all different types — requires its own classification system. The domain is essentially a reference book, a dictionary of abstractions, by analogy with business units in the org structure." (course day 3, transcript)
- "The domain structure is the skeleton of all data governance" — the board's slide lists what hangs off it: access matrix, DQ assessment, availability, info supply-demand matrix and communication plan, all *by domains*. (course day 2, transcript; course day 3, slides p.11, 40)
- Domains are needed even in a fully centralized model. There are simply far fewer expectations of them: approve the glossary, aggregate requirements, own access decisions, participate in certification and archiving — and explicitly no right to build their own datamarts. "You provide them the full service; you place X responsibilities on them and nothing more." (course day 2, transcript; course day 3, transcript)

### How to actually draw a domain structure
- Stop looking for the correct partitioning principle. "You can stare at your data forever trying to find the right splitting criterion and regroup it this way and that — and it usually has neither meaning nor benefit. The real value is just agreeing on a structure by common sense." (course day 3, transcript)
- The spreadsheet trick, stated exactly as he does it: open a sheet with three columns — domain, subdomain, responsible person. "As a rule, when you start filling in the responsible people, that is when you understand what the structure must be, because in real life it assembles itself." Fill names, not definitions. (course day 3, transcript)
- The failure test is physical, not conceptual: a bad domain structure "simply won't stretch onto your people." If you cannot name one person per cell without an argument, the cell is wrong — merge it, split it, or delete it. This is also the reason the exercise is cheap: it fails fast and visibly. (course day 3, transcript)
- The board's filled classifier from a large IT services company is the reference shape — a wide sheet with System → / Data domain → / Data Owner (Sign off) → / Business Data Steward → / R&A Data Steward → header bands, role groups down the side, and access levels in the cells. One artifact carries the domain map, the owner definition and the access matrix at once. (course day 3, slides p.23, 66-67)
- Where the answer is already written: "today any LLM will draft a typical domain structure better than I will. Feed it your warehouse schema — if you're not squeamish — and it maps it all for you." He closes the consulting service line explicitly: "I'd now solve this task not with consultants but with a chat model." Do the machine pass first; only the owner column then tells you whether that taxonomy corresponds to your organization. (course day 3, transcript)

### Why taxonomy debates are a trap
- The first tell: the discussion moves to criteria (by source system? by consumer? by business capability?) and stops producing names. Asked for industry-standard domain models, his answer was that a typical model is worth taking as a *base* to adapt — not as an object of debate. (course day 3, transcript)
- The second tell is the org-chart shortcut. "Domains are very often 90-95% close to the org-structure split, and there is always the temptation to essentially not define any domains at all — just to map object owners onto departments." That shortcut works right up to the moment a department reorganizes and your entire governance model is orphaned. (course day 3, transcript)
- The third tell is the business objection, which he lets stand unrebutted in the session. A participant running a commercial company: "Data is one world where we should all be stewing together — the more we bring them into contact with each other, the more value we get. What is the value of these domains for me? Okay, I appointed the HR director responsible. What is she supposed to actually *do*?" The honest answer is that domains are worth nothing until they carry duties; the value is in the responsibility routing, not the picture. (course day 3, transcript)
- The cross-check for whether you need domains at all comes from the drivers discussion: cross-functionality is the trigger word. If domains work in isolated contours and never need each other's data, "natural governance" suffices and losses do not appear. Domains earn their keep exactly where cross-domain use starts. (course day 2, transcript)

### Where domains begin, and where cross-domain entities live
- Domains begin at the warehouse raw layer, not at source systems. "Source systems usually participate poorly in our scheme. Domains begin, roughly speaking, at the warehouse, at the raw layer." Systems map to domains 1:1, 1:N and M:N; data from one system lands in several domains. Where the dependency is strong, you can pull that system's owner into the domain team — but that is the exception, not the model. (course day 3, transcript)
- In tech companies systems and domains often fuse into one vertical. When that happens the platform's job shrinks to tooling: catalog, checkers, DQ, training, a unified role vocabulary — "and then they understand it themselves and do it." (course day 3, transcript)
- Cross-domain entities get their own tier. The board reproduces a large ride-hailing company's three domain types: **Source** domains (structure fits the source; cleaning, dedup, standardization), **Core** domains (structure minimizes change; integration of many sources, surrogate keys), **Business** domains (structure fits consumers; effectively marts and reports) — and notes this is the same taxonomy as dbt's source-aligned / aggregate / consumer-aligned, i.e. the same three swim lanes as the mesh canvas on the board. (course day 3, slides p.16-18)
- The author's own gloss on the middle tier: "there are domains in the middle — a lot of data in, a lot of use cases out, and high standalone value. As a rule, client data. Or employee data." Client Master Data on the hexagon map is exactly this. The rule that follows: a cross-domain entity belongs to a core domain with its own owner, not to whichever consumer domain shouted first. (course day 3, transcript)
- The sovereignty fight is real and has a known counter. A domain declared "these are our data, we do what we want with them, no governance for us." Their CIO shut it down: "these are not your data — they are the whole business group's data. It is our common cause." (course day 3, transcript)

### Data products in practice
- The working definition is deliberately unromantic: a data product is "a mart meeting declared requirements — documented, with business checkers attached, healthy performance, architectural and code-quality compliance." At the author's company these are called core data objects / certified marts. (course day 1, transcript)
- Two tiers inside a domain, because one shape cannot serve both audiences: **tech core marts** (3NF, meant for building other marts) and **presentation core marts** (wide, convenient for analysts and BI queries). Within each domain, audit the architecture so source data is covered by a minimal, deduplicated set of products. (course day 1, transcript)
- Start with the register, not the architecture. "It is often enough that in some place — a registry, a catalog, or simply in Excel — you keep the set of marts you consider most significant, hang monitoring on them, assign a technical owner and a business owner, and watch that they all arrive intact every morning. For many companies that is already 80% of the governance they need." This is the same advice as the board's "simple register could be a good start". (course day 3, transcript)
- Reuse is the only honest adoption metric: count joins and reuse parameters of certified marts. Concrete targets from the core-layer program: share of user queries with 2+ joins 47% → 35% (2026) → 25% (2027); share of analyst queries hitting core tables 1% → 15% → 40%. (course day 1, transcript; course day 5, slides p.114)
- The reuse *contract* is what makes a data product a product. The core layer is built per horizontal domain — marketing first — "and then these core objects, these data products, must be used by everyone downstream": vertical teams are expected to reuse the horizontal's marts instead of rebuilding them. "In practice, because of the high decentralization, this is very difficult in real life and is not solved by administrative measures." (course day 5, transcript)
- The staffing trap to plan around: in roughly 80% of companies the core layer will be built by data engineers, "who by nature are not product people" — expect product-ownership problems and budget for them. (course day 3, transcript)

### The centralization slider
- "Everyone sits on a centralization–decentralization slider, and the slider position — not the fashion — is the key strategic decision" of the DWH/platform head: it determines your roles system, your RACI, what analysts and engineers are allowed to do on the platform, your stack, even architecture fit. The board's five operational models are the slider drawn out. (course day 2, transcript)
- The DG fork appears exactly at the mesh end: "you give the platform out in self-service — tools and rules of behaviour — and after that you're weak, and you watch people create nonsense on the platform." At the centralized end "you don't let outsiders onto the platform; all the people are in one organization and you can make them do what you need." Governance problems are the same in both; only the cost of enforcement differs. (course day 2, transcript)
- Terminology hygiene he insists on: data mesh is an org model (plus governance), NOT a storage architecture. You can run Data Vault with a centralized team and borrow mesh elements; Lakehouse/DWH is the tech layer. Confusing the two is widespread "even among IT specialists". And maturity ≠ decentralization: decentralization arises because a complex platform makes centralization a bottleneck, but a simpler centralized platform can be perfectly mature for a retail company. There is no single ladder here. (course day 2, transcript)
- The centralized counterpoint, conceded fully: for regulatory and management reporting, with end-to-end control of the chain, a centralized model reaches a level of quality unreachable in a mesh. A large IT services company took ~8 years to build fully controlled executive reporting — "such quality of reporting control as I have never met in any other company since." (course day 2, transcript)

### The case against mesh-first
- The rule, stated as a settled opinion: "any data mesh must be built starting from centralization. Then, once the processes and approaches are built out, you decentralize the central platform team a little and hand out that freedom — because the engineers working in the domains will keep the platform culture we want from them." Seed the domains from the platform; don't seed the platform from the domains. (course day 2, transcript)
- His company did the opposite, and he says so plainly: "in our case it was not like that — we had maximum Wild West immediately: decentralization, full freedom, and the governance activities never took root. We're still cleaning that up now." (course day 2, transcript)
- What the freedom actually bought: "the culture — big-tech IT culture — presumes a high degree of freedom and trust. In our case that creates very many problems." Every team along the chain accumulates tech debt and "creates nonsense", because nothing in the model penalizes it. The structural verdict follows: "the negative all comes down to this — a decentralized approach with a high degree of domain autonomy from the start leads to chaos, and the governance that is supposed to exist alongside it does not take off. The platform's influence over the domains is very weak in this scheme by design." (course day 5, transcript)
- And the trap closes: "once you have let the domains go and they've sailed away, your ability to influence them starts to fall too. You have to roll back and raise the platform's influence over a pile of things — which means slowing TTM and taking away some freedom." The available instruments are indirect: program the platform's behaviour in the tooling, or catch inefficiency through product metrics — health scores, join counts, platform analytics. (course day 5, transcript)
- The "tax" framing, given in answer to a participant asking why all this scaffolding is needed at all: **"this is the tax of a big platform."** The core data of such a platform are written by hundreds of products and services, dozens and dozens of independent teams need them, "and in that sense we generate a great deal of inefficiency." (course day 5, transcript)
- Authority for the scepticism is not his alone: "the founder of data mesh herself is now rewriting the topic a bit." (course day 5, transcript)
- The honest counter-question, which he refuses to dodge: could the company have built it right from the start? "There's a good chance it couldn't — at the growth stage they had different priorities. Companies reach the consolidation point and are then forced to think retroactively." A participant sharpens it into the accusation worth remembering: "so it's a crutch — we architected it wrong at the start and then propped it up with governance, hoping it'll work now." (course day 5, transcript)
- Self-assessment of his own mesh: "we say we have an à-la-data-mesh architecture, but of the four components we really only have one — the platform. No self-service, no proper domain structure, no federated governance. That is what we still want to mesh and build." (course day 1, transcript)
- One thing must stay central even in a mesh: base checker coverage and incident generation. "Delegating that to the domains is a risky story — it simply won't start." (course day 4, transcript)

### What happened in the companies that went mesh-first
- A large ride-hailing tech company broke up its platform and distributed data engineers into domains to own their core layers and marts. Four years later the peer-status board records the outcome: "after distributing engineers into domains, failed to make the data partners proactive — they stayed reactive." (course day 2, transcript; course day 6, slides p.107)
- A large fintech suspended its DG program after a CDO change, with priority given to decentralization and data mesh; stewards had only ever been centralized, and "regulation and role-imposition are rejected by the culture" — de-facto acceptance of the roles never started. Later the same company cut its whole DG team, stewards and catalog support included. (course day 3, slides p.72; course day 2, transcript)
- A large e-commerce player: no DG program at all, "DG dissolved in processes", six domain teams — and the domain isolation that follows kills data sharing. Their own anti-catalog argument is the mesh end-state in one sentence: "we have no calm period for DG-type projects; everyone works inside their own domain and doesn't go outside — there's no access there anyway, so chats are enough for us." (course day 2, slides p.54; course day 3, slides p.72)
- His own company appears in the same research row with "DG/DQ team inside the data platform but no DG program, CDO not connected to the data platform, analyst roles in DG not formalized, **no domains**" — the domain gap named explicitly as a gap. (course day 3, slides p.72)
- The counter-example that keeps the picture honest: a mature large fintech running a heavily layered platform alongside many self-service ETL sandboxes with deliberately lighter governance for urgent business work, while core-model changes go through multi-day reviewed pull requests. Cost of the balance: a large shadow ETL — production processes running on self-service objects. (course day 2, transcript)

### Sandbox / prod balance
- Environment design as the practical middle of the slider: separate archive / sandbox / prod. Sandboxes carry lower documentation and health requirements but hard restrictions — no sharing objects outside the team, no schedules. "You don't cut a person's rights, but you keep the environment sane for everyone else." The whole game is the balance between what you block at commit time and what you detect afterwards. (course day 2, transcript)

### Metric ownership (case from a real-estate classified)
- Assigning ALL metrics to product teams failed. Responsibility for a metric is distributed across functions (CPT revenue touches Monetization, Pricing, Sales and Finance; audience metrics touch every product line plus Marketing and CRM); cross-team coordination costs more than teams will pay; and data analysts turn out to be bad at writing methodology — "strong pushback on such tasks; it needs a business/systems-analyst skill set." (course day 5, slides p.31)
- The working split: key metrics (top-level, in the company business model, cross-team, externally reported) → Data Steward, and that is where DG focus must be; local product metrics (drivers) → Data Analyst, accumulated systematically but second priority. Chain: metric registry → classify the key ones → assign owners → describe methodology → business glossary. Scope: one domain pair per year. (course day 5, slides p.32-33)

### How domain structure feeds AI context
- The enterprise data ontology — "a mix of domain structure and glossary" — is getting a second life with LLMs as the substrate for RAG in internal assistants. The slide's own caption on the messy real-world ontology map is the instruction: "what matters to us here is to arrive at the domain structure." One more reason to invest in domains now rather than later. (course day 3, slides p.28-30; transcript)
- The AI-Ready Domain checklist is the concrete deliverable, and it is a domain artifact end to end: domain boundary and owner in 1-3 phrases with an escalation route; markup of certified datamarts, metrics and dashboards; short meta per key object (what it computes, and "don't use for..." limitations); typical "how do I...?" Q&A scenarios with an answer and an example link; pointers to adjacent domains ("for X ask domain Y"); a domain glossary. Responsibility split: Domain Owner (Head of Analytics) / BI Partner (marts and dashboards) / Metric Curator (metrics). (course day 6, slides p.95)
- Domain identity is also a retrieval filter: the assistant personalizes by the asker's domain, filtering objects even unprompted — so a domain structure that maps to people maps directly to agent precision. Domain glossaries assemble bottom-up from tagged pages where teams already describe their own specifics and methodology; no company-wide glossary needed first. (course day 6, transcript)
- The budget argument this creates: with no dedicated DG FTE to defend, the defence runs through AI — "under all our agents there must be a core data layer, a semantic layer and certified reports, and certification can only be achieved communally." AI-readiness of domains becomes the shield for domain governance. (course day 3, transcript)

## Maturity signals
Observable markers, in rough order of appearance:
- **The owner column fills in without an argument.** The first and cheapest signal: the structure stretches onto the people. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Domains and roles are mastered in the catalog, not in Excel.** "The master system for domains and roles is the data catalog. Role assignment, re-assignment and steward onboarding must be normalized with alerts" — otherwise people wash out silently and "we discovered nobody had been tending a domain for half a year." (course day 3, transcript)
- **Owner coverage is measured per domain and is embarrassing at first.** A real Purview screenshot across domain collections: 4-45% of assets with an owner assigned, 46-74% with no classification. A job-search platform's custom catalog: only 30% of objects have owners. Show the number to the sponsor — it is the baseline. (course day 3, slides p.24; course day 4, slides p.87)
- **The domain becomes the reporting unit.** DQ scorecard by domain (DWH-model coverage vs "knocked-together" coverage, DQ status, resource invested, next-year priority); sensitivity-classification heatmap by domain; criticality tables by domain. And the domain partner has a cabinet, not a spreadsheet — a per-domain page in the tooling showing their critical objects, health scores and open problems. (course day 1, slides p.76-77; course day 3, slides p.24-25, 34; transcript)
- **2-3 domains are visibly ahead and are used as the example.** "With 10-20 domains you'll always find 2-3 proactive ones — start there; the rest get worn down by success posts." The mirror signal is honest naming of outsiders: "there are domain leaders who are a joy to take as an example, and there are outsiders where nothing works at all." (course day 3, slides p.88; transcript)
- **Governance is inside the domain partners' goals, not asked as a favour.** ~20% of BI partners' time reserved through an agreed "tech debt includes governance" rule; the competency matrix changed so they cannot pass calibration without a defined set of governance activities. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Domain partners are proactive rather than reactive.** The explicit failure marker from the peer research; this is the one thing distributing engineers into domains did *not* achieve. (course day 6, slides p.107)
- **Reuse moves.** Joins per query falling, share of queries hitting core tables rising. Until reuse moves, "data products" are a naming convention. (course day 5, slides p.114)
- **The info supply-demand matrix is filled** — domains/subdomains × management roles, marking who needs what — and drives the report portfolio rather than sitting in a deck. (course day 3, slides p.26)
- **Domains show up in the assistant.** Agent retrieval filtered by domain, certified objects marked per domain, "don't use for..." notes written. (course day 6, slides p.95; transcript)

## Anti-patterns
- **Taxonomy paralysis.** Months of debate over the splitting criterion, zero names assigned. Cure: fill the owner column first; "the real value is just agreeing on a structure by common sense." (course day 3, transcript)
- **Mesh-first.** Freedom and autonomy handed out before processes, culture and platform practices exist, on the promise that federated governance will follow. It does not: "the governance that is supposed to exist alongside it does not take off." Recovery costs TTM and political capital. (course day 2, transcript; course day 5, transcript)
- **The org-chart copy.** Skipping domains entirely and mapping object owners onto departments. Survives until the next reorg. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Domains without owners.** A published domain map with empty accountability cells — the Purview 4-45% picture. The structure exists as a diagram and governs nothing. (course day 3, slides p.24)
- **Structure that doesn't map to people.** Elegant taxonomy, no candidate for the responsible person; the domain "won't stretch onto your people" and quietly stops being used. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Data sovereignty.** "These are our data, we do what we want." Needs an executive counter-statement, not a policy document. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Business executives as domain data owners.** Draping data-quality duties on a CFO or HR director yields pushback and a nominal role; if the domain has a head of analytics, hang ownership there. (course day 1, transcript; course day 3, transcript)
- **Full metric devolution.** Handing every metric to product teams, ignoring that a metric's responsibility is distributed across functions; coordination cost exceeds what teams will pay. (course day 5, slides p.31)
- **Decentralizing the DQ floor.** Delegating base checker coverage and incident generation to domains — "it simply won't start." (course day 4, transcript)
- **Data products owned by nobody product-minded.** Core layer built by data engineers with no product ownership planned for — the default in ~80% of companies. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Governance as retrofit.** The participant's formulation, worth keeping as a warning label: "we architected it wrong at the start and then propped it up with governance." (course day 5, transcript)

## Questions to ask when designing domain structure
- **Where does your self-service line sit?** "The whole question is where you draw the point of your self-service, of your data platform." Everything else — expectations of domains, role model, RACI — follows from that answer. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Can you name a person for every cell, today, without a meeting?** If not, the structure is wrong, not the people. (course day 3, transcript)
- **What do you actually expect this domain to do?** Glossary approval and requirements only? Or ingestion, cleaning, transformation, DQ, reporting and documentation? Write the list before you draw the map. (course day 2, transcript; course day 3, transcript)
- **Do your source systems map 1:1 or M:N?** If M:N — the usual case — accept that source teams participate poorly and start the domain at the raw layer. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Which entities are genuinely cross-domain, and which core domain will own them?** Client, employee, and the other "middle" entities with many inputs and many use cases. (course day 3, transcript; slides p.16-18)
- **Is there real cross-functional use, or are you building domains for isolated contours?** No cross-domain use, no losses, no case. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Are you at the growth stage or the consolidation point?** Freedom is cheap in growth and expensive afterwards; the slider position should be a conscious answer to this question, not an inherited one. (course day 5, transcript)
- **Which 2-3 domains do you start with, and who are the visible outsiders?** Phase stewardship: one area → prepare the steward → work through the problems → let success spread. (course day 3, slides p.88)
- **Has an LLM already drafted this?** Feed it the warehouse schema before booking the workshop; spend the human time on owners, not on boxes. (course day 3, transcript)
- **Where will the structure be mastered a year from now?** Catalog with re-assignment alerts, or a spreadsheet nobody re-opens. (course day 3, transcript)

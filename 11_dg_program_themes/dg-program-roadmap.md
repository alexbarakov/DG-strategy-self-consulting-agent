# DG Program Roadmap

## What the board teaches
The master artifact is the "Data / AI Governance Program Map 3.0": the DG journey staged 0-5 — Basic Concepts (0) → is DG needed at all (1) → framework, goals and buy-in (2, with sub-tracks 2.1 Common Sense DG and 2.2 DG MVP) → launch (3) → projects in the most critical streams (4: catalog, certification/data products, DQM, data ops, privacy/security, data literacy, stewardship) → framework development (5: operating model, cost-benefit, maturity, non-invasive DG, data strategy). Each stage carries explicit *fail branches*: "driver not found", "D&A maturity too low", "overestimating your determination", "pseudo DG — only a catalog, no business involvement". Two roadmap tools complete it: a 12-stream × 4 half-year grid (61 task cards, recurring goal/domain-structure refinement checkpoints), and the "land and expand" roadmap sequencing initiatives into Stage 1 Essential Minimum → Stage 2 Reasonable Addition → Stage 3 Matured Focus, with sponsorship gates (C-level 1/4 then 2/4) and a Budget Denial loop back to Common Sense DG + MVP restart. Throughout: stage order is a generalization, improvise, and return to your simplified framework when overwhelmed.

## Key objects
- Program Map 3.0 stages 0-5 (above); Stage 4 streams carry 15-25 hexagon tasks each
- Fail branches: no driver / low maturity; value drivers insufficient for buy-in; insufficient determination against resistance; "pseudo DG" (catalog-only)
- 12 roadmap streams: preparation/goal-setting; own DG solution/framework; operational model; data catalog; data quality; data stewardship; privacy compliance; data access management; data certification; data literacy; data culture; other initiatives — each across 4 half-year stages
- Recurring checkpoints: refine goals/roadmap; refine domain structure, role model, processes + audit of critical assets
- Land-and-expand stages: Stage 1 Essential Minimum (domain structure, metric tree/ontology, lifecycle standardization, importance classification, roles, documentation by criticality, incident management, DQM coverage + public DQ status); Stage 2 Reasonable Addition (golden layer, PII classification, data contracts); Stage 3 Matured Focus (glossary, access automation, semantic layer and metric store)
- Budget Denial path → ongoing Common Sense DG + MVP restart; expansion triggers: MVP domain owner, interest from other domains, CDO/CTO/CFO/CEO support
- Manifesto of an effective DG leader: truly believe in DG; implement practically; explain DG concisely; never start without IT and business stakeholder support
- Data cleaning misconception: up to 70% of cleaning effort wasted (McKinsey); clean for critical use cases while building ontology and core data model

## From the course (Data Governance Fundamentals, 6 days)

### Sequencing: why order beats content
- The "Rational way to launch DG" flowchart is the spine: Start ("Do we need a formal / dedicated data governance?") → MVP project team → pain & driver analysis → choice of first domain → *either* Common Sense DG *or* DG MVP → package MVP results into a case → full pain analysis + program concept + cost-benefit model + sponsor buy-in → budget fork → land and expand. Everything left of the budget fork is reachable without money. (course day 6, slides p.100)
- The Stage 1/2/3 colouring is a *speed-of-payback* ordering, not a completeness ordering — "my estimate of the ordering, based on how fast the benefit arrives and how labour-intensive it is." Mis-ordering is negative, not merely late: "the cost of individual initiatives can be higher than their value at a given maturity, company size and business specifics — then there is no sense in starting them until something changes." (course day 6, slides p.100-101)
- The load-bearing dependency runs upward: "for a BI report to be certified you need to certify the datamart under it; to certify the datamart you need data contracts, data checks, health — and a role model under that." Hence certification is sequenced marts → reports → metrics, even though reports feel closer to the user. (course day 2, transcript; day 3, transcript)
- A rule that saves months: "don't waste time on excessive work with business if you can start endogenously, on platform resources. Business will never understand the full scope of the problem you are solving." (course day 6, transcript)
- The 12-stream grid is a parallelism map, not a Gantt: goal-setting and "development of your own DG framework" run together in Stage 1, because the domain structure is the framework's base and the role model must be conceived up front — "it matters what the role model will be, depending on your platform model, centralization or decentralization." Catalog market analysis begins *before* the official start so procurement doesn't become the critical path. (course day 6, slides p.103; transcript)
- Which stream leads after launch is not a methodological choice: "either you go all-in on compliance, if compliance is your driver, or into DQM, or into certification, or into data literacy." The driver picks the stream. (course day 6, transcript)
- Domain structure comes first because it is cheap and makes everything after it assignable — "the domain structure is the skeleton of all data governance"; a bad structure "simply won't stretch onto your people". (course day 2, transcript; day 3, slides p.11)

### Picking the first domains
- The flowchart's instruction: "choice of the most critical business domain — or one of the significant data objects (client...)". Not the easiest, not the loudest — the one where the driver lives. (course day 6, slides p.100)
- The counterweight from the stewardship track: "with 10-20 domains you will always find 2-3 proactive ones — start there; the rest get worn down by success posts." (course day 3, slides p.88)
- Cian's scoping discipline: one domain *pair* per year (2024 secondary + primary real estate, 2025 UX + transactions), chained metric registry → classify key metrics → assign owners → describe methodology → glossary. It worked because it was narrow. (course day 5, slides p.32-33)
- The MVP team shape is specified, not left to taste: 2-3 leads/seniors from DWH/BI teams, systems thinkers, 2+ years inside the company. Tenure is a selection criterion. (course day 6, slides p.100)
- Smallest viable first scope worth stealing (Lean DG): 1 problem statable in one sentence, 1 domain, 3-10 datasets, 3 roles, 3 automated checks, one-page docs, weekly 30-min data office hours, quarterly expansion to the next 3 datasets. (course day 1, slides p.56, 119)

### The budget fork
- Base rate: "90% of projects never get that budget." The no-budget branch is the *main* branch — which is why the course is built as a dichotomy, luxury maximum vs base minimum. (course day 5, transcript; day 2, transcript)
- The expansion gate is quantified: minimum 2 of 4 C-level sponsors (CDO/CTO/CFO/CEO), plus the MVP domain owner, plus demonstrated interest from other domain owners. Below that you have a project, not a program. (course day 6, slides p.100)
- On refusal the board loops back deliberately: continue Common Sense DG, restart the MVP later. "Instead of starting a big program you create a set of projects in different teams — sometimes unconnected, but each solving a concrete task... data governance without any formal wrapper. And they can be effective at your maturity stage." (course day 6, slides p.100; day 2, transcript)
- The inverted decision — sometimes you should not ask: "once you have a program with a budget, the question arises whether you pay it back... if your company watches money very closely, it may be better not to form a separate DG team at all, and instead hide this work inside teams that have a clearer reason to exist." Reinforced by: "by honest calculation my whole DG slice brings the company ~11M RUB/month — very little for [a company this size]. I'm still deciding whether to show that number to anyone." (course day 5, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- Sponsorship management is explicitly *not* Stage 1 on the land-and-expand grid: "some things can be done without your own standalone sponsorship — riding the sponsorship of other projects. Finding separate sponsors for DG will be needed when you go further, when you need committees and large teams." Sponsorship is not a hygiene factor. (course day 6, transcript)

### Selling it, and the business case
- Alfa-Bank DQ practice (Andrey Zavarzin, 2011): nobody has ever seen the textbook loss-vs-cost optimum curve in real life; top managers care about exactly two numbers — agreed possible losses and the cost of works preventing them. "A positive start decision is always made when minimal losses exceed maximal costs." (course day 1, slides p.44-45)
- Going to business under the literal flag "Data Governance" fails in ~95% of cases — the word evokes "distant and secondary". Package it under something else. (course day 2, transcript)
- Central thesis: "It is useless to explain DG to the business and wait for enthusiastic support. Business will support you but will never postpone its own tasks for DG tickets. DG is a thing data/analytics leaders must arrive at themselves. The maturity of your own team in key positions decides." (course day 6, slides p.104)
- Method: map data & analytics use cases onto the company's value-adding process flows, score impact × feasibility; when ~20 cases each individually "doable without DG" accumulate, together they prove systemic problems worth a strategy. Attribution honesty: the only truly *direct* DG economy is infrastructure savings; everything else is indirect. Better: read the company strategy and hang DG under initiatives that already have money attached. (course day 2, slides p.47-49; transcript)
- SOFTSWISS — the rare genuine case, and note its sequencing: pains from dCOO/CFO/dCPO first, then 2 FTE investigating data and reports for one quarter, then a day-by-day breakdown of the month-end ISR closing process (9 → 6 business days on the invoicing step), invoice deltas from 100 EUR to >100k EUR, 2-3 excess workdays monthly across 11 finance employees. Head of Internal Accounting confirmed the value; they went to the committee with an internal auditor as witness. A 3-person DG team was funded off an Excel of small monetized cases. The slide title carries the lesson: "DG as a service that helps start / unblock business initiatives." (course day 2, slides p.41-43; day 6, slides p.108-110)

### Vision and goals
- DG Vision = a 4-6-page Amazon-style six-pager: problem analysis from drivers/pains, target framework concept, goals, metrics, projects, roadmap; refresh after each delivery cycle — "in the end it may be the only document you actually need." (course day 2, slides p.50; day 6, transcript)
- Goals in three tiers: program goals (slogan-ish), DG team goals (SMART, annual), steward goals (">=98% of new product records accurate at entry"). Don't write goals you can't measure ("transparency", "trust") — use reuse rate, DQ metrics, search speed. (course day 2, slides p.56-57)
- Every canvas in the course is subtractive: the first move on the goals configurator is to *delete* goals and initiatives not relevant to you; the maturity canvas says "your maturity starts with removing what doesn't apply"; the BI Strategy canvas says "delete everything that doesn't fit your company's maturity." (course day 2, slides p.57; day 6, slides p.124)
- The framework's real function is focus retention "for boring but systematic movement over the course of a year", not control — "maybe a bit of bureaucracy won't hurt us now." Data Strategy appears organically *after* DG start, not as a prerequisite. (course day 5, slides p.64; day 6, slides p.121)

### Launching without a budget
- Packaging trick: relabel an existing stream of DQ projects as "DG working group + committee" and invite sponsors from platform, HR and analytics — "ordinary work, packaged so it can be tracked" and reported upward. (course day 5, slides p.64; transcript)
- Without a DG office you assemble the program piecemeal — extend dashboard certification into mart certification with the Head of DWH, split the project between teams; documentation then exists per-component, not per-program, and that's fine. (course day 5, transcript)
- The staffing arithmetic: 0.2-0.3 FTE per person distributed across teams; centres of expertise per governance slice form naturally "because nobody has the resource to own it all — you find the right team, sell them the idea, and land it in their goals." (course day 5, transcript)
- But distribution alone doesn't converge: "the teams will hardly coordinate the pieces of a DG program by themselves. Someone must be the visionary." Usually the Core BI / cross-functional analytics team, named a critical success factor and data curator in "weak" domains. (course day 5, transcript; day 6, slides p.105)
- "Invisible / non-invasive DG": chain initiatives that pull each other (core layer pulls DQ checks, checks pull the catalog, domains pull roles) — works, but "much slower than with a focused resource." (course day 6, transcript)
- Run an MVP even when approval is easy: a large marketplace shipped dashboard certification in 3 months plus a data-cleanup subbotnik — both moved metrics AND worked emotionally, buying license for more expensive initiatives. (course day 6, slides p.104; transcript)

### Dependency deadlocks
- **Governance vs transformation.** Ozon: "process transformation every quarter — there is no calm period for DG projects." A permanent reorg cadence is itself a blocker; pick initiatives that fit inside one quarter. (course day 2, slides p.54; day 6, slides p.107)
- **Governance vs product delivery inside the platform.** "In the fight for engineers between platform product streams, the catalog loses." Every catalog surveyed shows a "fading trend" because almost nobody justifies a permanent team for it. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Catalog vs everything else.** Catalog value is hostage to self-service BI, data products, contracts, ownership and DQM maturing — "do you have enough cumulative maturity to launch a catalog and get value from it?" If not, redirect the resource there. (course day 4, slides p.42-44)
- **Glossary vs semantic layer.** The glossary deadlocks because it needs cross-domain facilitation with business experts *and* a synchronous semantic-layer project ("hello, dbt metrics"). Two hard dependencies at once is why "in big techs the glossary simply doesn't take off." (course day 5, slides p.28)
- **Governance vs freedom already granted.** Once domains have sailed away, "winning influence back means slowing time-to-market and taking freedom away." (course day 5, transcript)
- **Shift-left**, the longest and most valuable chain: certified dashboards → forces datamart quality → forces raw-layer quality → forces contracts with source systems → forces product thinking on system owners. For backend teams "data is exhaust — they need to ship features." Breaking that is what a CDO's authority is for. (course day 2, transcript)

### How programs die quietly
- **Organizational dissolution.** At a large telecom ecosystem the DG team was elevated to ecosystem level with ~20 stewards, then "dissolved organizationally — it ate a lot of money, internal centers of power over data emerged and competed for budgets; DG practice, methodology and the data platform reported to three different VPs; some parts got terminated." (course day 1, transcript)
- **Sponsor rotation.** At Tinkoff the program was suspended after the CDO changed. A program depending on one executive has a half-life equal to that executive's tenure. (course day 3, slides p.72)
- **Silent budget cut.** A large fintech recently cut its entire DG team — stewards and catalog support. "They'll probably resuscitate it later." (course day 2, transcript)
- **Role evaporation.** Stewards wash out without an event; the master system for domains and roles must be the catalog with alerts on re-assignment, "otherwise we discover nobody had been tending a domain for half a year." (course day 3, transcript)
- **Tool without process.** Tinkoff's DataHealer is flagged red — "used voluntarily, without control or goals"; the author's own DQ module gets the same verdict, "the tool exists = the process doesn't." (course day 4, slides p.145)
- **Documentation decay**, structural rather than a discipline failure: even the author's own BI strategy went stale between writing and socialization — "it looks like I have to write it anew." (course day 5, transcript)
- The author's own defence is to leave no single point to cut: "there was no dedicated DG FTE to cut — DG is smeared across teams." (course day 3, transcript)

### Cadence and review mechanics
- Bodies map onto a cadence, not a hierarchy: DG Council roughly monthly, Steering Committee roughly quarterly — and "don't call them committees; 'data management sync' sounds less bureaucratic and works the same." Health check: 80-85% of conflicts should resolve at business-unit stewardship level, <20% reach the Council, <5% the Steering Committee. (course day 5, slides p.65, 68; transcript)
- Monthly steward ritual that works without formal authority: a meeting with domain BI partners showing dashboards with per-steward metrics — "peer dynamics alone motivates even before you get the right to set targets." (course day 5, transcript)
- Meeting hygiene: expect stewards to ask "should I keep attending?" after the very first session — have a ready answer and repeat it every time; if someone's honest answer is "no", admit it; never meet for meeting's sake; close by listing the day's achievements. (course day 3, slides p.86-87)
- The half-yearly checkpoints in the 12-stream grid are refinement, not status: refine goals and roadmap, refine domain structure / role model / processes, audit critical assets. Stage 5 adds the one most programs skip — "sober roadmap refinement: abandon ineffective practices, analyse capacity." (board, Program Map 3.0; 12-stream roadmap frame)
- The strongest annual mechanism is not a review but a goal: the company adopts a maturity model, each top manager gets an annual goal "function reaches level X", and steward duties cascade themselves. "Maturity models work when a company appropriates one." (course day 3, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- Review *content* should migrate, Allianz-style: from quantity of governance (# standards, # stewards, # glossary terms) to business impact (% call-centre calls caused by data defects, % manual processes with automated validations, % reserves held due to data issues). "What we governed 10 years ago is not what we want to govern now." (course day 6, slides p.30-31)

### How the AI era changes the sequencing
- Urgency clause: "The feeling is that a dam has been opened. One quarter of chasing efficiency is enough to flood the whole valley. Governance must be thought through. Immediately." And the sober half — "after the 'Wild West' strategy comes the counting of burned tokens." (course day 6, slides p.96)
- AI moved the budget hook: "for the AI model to work you need to feed it quality data — surprise." The catalog is argued as the proto-base of AI context, funding both catalog and role model. In cost cuts the same argument is the shield: "under all our agents there must be a core data layer, a semantic layer and certified reports — and certification can only be achieved communally." (course day 2, transcript; day 3, transcript)
- Certification jumped forward in the ordering: the agent navigates users to certified objects first, so certification became an AI prerequisite rather than a bureaucratic ritual. "Trust sprawl happened inside the semantic layer too." Agents are named the next certification frontier. (course day 5, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- What AI unblocked is exactly what used to sit in Stage 3 — glossary and ontology. The historical blocker "was never technology but coordination of people and intellectual effort across many teams." A Central Bank glossary of 10k+ terms went from ~9 months of manual work to an AI prototype capturing ~80%; implementation time-to-market is "an order of magnitude faster", which used to be the #1 stop-factor. (course day 5, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- What AI did *not* change: the sale. A vendor's chat-with-your-data over all metadata layers excited prospects and generated zero purchases — "the concrete loop 'do this and your business runs better' is still not assembled." (course day 6, transcript)
- The bottleneck moves one step downstream every time you automate. A domain steward built business-logic DQ checkers for 160 datamarts in one day (normally ~3 weeks); the checkers then flooded incidents, requiring an incident-management agent plus an eval-agent judging checker adequacy. "The boost survives after subtracting validation costs, but it's smaller than the first emotions." Plan the next roadmap stage for the bottleneck you are about to create. (course day 6, transcript)
- New roadmap objects absent from earlier versions of the map: a registry/catalog of AI agents and skills with certification and deletion of bad ones; marking corporate content for what agents may ingest; agent access registered separately from user access; observability over agent actions. (course day 6, slides p.93, 96; transcript)
- The AI-Ready Domain checklist is effectively the new per-domain Stage 1 exit criterion: boundary + owner in 1-3 phrases with escalation route, markup of certified marts/metrics/dashboards, short meta per key object including "don't use for...", typical "how do I...?" Q&A with links, pointers to adjacent domains, domain glossary — assembled bottom-up from tagged Confluence pages, no company-wide glossary needed first. (course day 6, slides p.95; transcript)
- Anti-FOMO clause for the roadmap: "nobody is late; laggard companies will enter in 2-5 years when the practices commoditize", and AI growth is throttled by compute cost — "if AI costs more than a human, automation stops being profitable." (course day 6, slides p.97)

## Maturity signals
Which stage a company is *actually* at, as opposed to which one its deck claims.

- **Ownership coverage.** Across Azure Purview domain collections only 4-45% of assets had owners and 46-74% lacked classifications — "a typical starting picture worth showing to sponsors." hh.ru's Atlas catalog: 30% of objects have owners. Below half, you are Stage 1-2 whatever the catalog page says. (course day 3, slides p.24; day 4, slides p.87)
- **DQ coverage of critical objects.** hh: "datachecks cover ~10% of critical objects, no golden layer, no certification." That triple is the signature of a Stage 1 company with a Stage 4 deck. (course day 6, slides p.107)
- **Tool exists vs process exists.** "The tool exists = the process doesn't." A deployed DQ engine or catalog proves procurement maturity, nothing else. (course day 4, slides p.145)
- **Where the platform team hides.** "The data platform team hides behind the data catalog and avoids the hard data-management questions" — in particular the role model. If the catalog answers every governance question, the role model doesn't exist. (course day 6, slides p.99)
- **Role adoption vs role assignment.** Tinkoff: "acceptance of roles de facto never happened, nobody there even started." Yandex Taxi: after distributing engineers into domains, "failed to make data partners proactive — they stayed reactive." Assignment is free; proactivity is the signal. (course day 6, slides p.107)
- **Glossary status.** Yandex Taxi wanted one but needs business capacity; Tinkoff never built one; VK not in focus; hh "sort of" maintained. A live, re-reviewed glossary is the loudest Stage 3 signal precisely because almost nobody has one. (course day 5, slides p.28)
- **Do you know your own funnel?** Avito's shape to compare against: Redash 12,800 dashboards → 11,500 outside sandboxes → 4,100 in use → ~150 (now ~400) key reports; m42 15,182 metrics → 4,604 important → 1,464 in use. Having the funnel at all is a maturity signal. (course day 3, slides p.103)
- **Industry baseline.** Eckerson/RateMyData: average DG maturity 2.88/5.0 ("Initiating"); only 2% of programs deliver high business value, 22% deliver none; top challenges lack of stewards 54%, conflicting priorities 46%, lack of a plan 46%. (course day 6, slides p.40-43)
- **How effectiveness is talked about.** "Effectiveness evidence across the industry is mostly emotional" — Yandex Taxi: the catalog "gave positive vibes"; VK: "entropy is decreasing." A company that actually ran the analysis (search time with vs without catalog × requests × analyst FTE cost) is further along than one with a nicer framework diagram. (course day 6, slides p.34)
- **Which instrument to use.** The author's pick of open models is the UK Government Data Maturity Framework — 10 topics, 97 criteria, "neither overloaded nor superficial". Mechanics: no single person holds the whole picture, so either ~20 interviews or a facilitated session with stickers and voting; mailed questionnaires must be radically simplified or nobody answers. Shortcut: feed the model to an LLM, have it interview you (~30 questions) and produce the positioning report. (course day 6, slides p.44-45; transcript)

## Anti-patterns
- **Pseudo DG.** The board's Stage 3 fail branch: only the catalog gets implemented, no business involvement. A catalog "becomes a DG tool only when the steward has an equipped workplace in it" — a task inbox, policies to approve, checks to review, rights to re-review. Otherwise it is a search box. (board, Program Map 3.0; course day 1, transcript)
- **Burnt desert.** Paying for a catalog and getting ghost towns of empty descriptions; trust drops and the tool degrades. The fix is sequencing, not effort: automated filling, algorithmic pre-fill of domain/stewards/tags, descriptions inherited up the lineage, docs entered in the flow of work via a CI/CD gate, curating only the top 20% node objects. (course day 4, slides p.40, 46-47)
- **Data-first cleansing.** Up to 70% of cleansing effort wasted (McKinsey); a large company spent hundreds of millions and 2+ years on an enterprise-wide cleansing/data-lake initiative that largely failed "because nobody knew which data served which use cases." (course day 3, slides p.9)
- **Glossary-first.** "Reserved for the mature" — "there is never resource for it, and physical/logical-level processes matter more than the conceptual layer." Attempting it first burns the political capital you need for everything else. (course day 6, transcript)
- **Semantic layer as an entry ticket.** "Don't assume you must have it — it may simply not be affordable for you." The heuristic is a headcount question: count the BI devs/analysts hand-coding business logic; if they don't overlap much, keep writing SQL. (course day 6, slides p.100; day 5, transcript)
- **Decentralize first, govern later.** "Immediately maximum Wild West — full decentralization, freedom, and governance activities never took root; we're still cleaning that up." The prescription is the inverse: build the mesh starting from centralization, then hand out freedom. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Metrics assigned to product teams wholesale.** Cian tried it and it failed: responsibility for a metric is distributed across functions, cross-team coordination costs more than teams will pay, and "data analysts are bad at writing methodology and requirements." Working split: key metrics → Data Steward, local product metrics → Data Analyst. (course day 5, slides p.31-33)
- **Business data stewards as the load-bearing role.** "Business people will never do hands-on steward work in any workflow you design." Design the roadmap around technical stewards / custodians / data partners. (course day 2, transcript; day 3, slides p.78)
- **Approach without adoption.** "'We developed an approach but they don't follow it' — kindergarten." All key DG initiatives are cross-role; the DG leader's job description is mediator. (course day 6, slides p.105)
- **Hiring the DG leader from the market.** "You'll wait years for him to become an old-timer — nobody lets a newcomer seriously change their processes. You get sabotage and imitation." (course day 6, slides p.104)
- **Org chart before value.** DAMA's full body stack is "a maximum program. Without necessity, build a smaller-scale governance system"; real committees exist in "maybe two dozen companies max" in Russia. Small orgs should explicitly skip local councils. (course day 5, slides p.56; transcript)
- **The policy spiral.** Each new regulation, data type and self-service wave adds policies → worse navigation → lower compliance. Answer: new policies only when needed, archive when stale, push policy into tools "so the policy itself becomes unnecessary." (course day 6, slides p.118-119)
- **Selling decision speed.** "Attempts to measure decision-making acceleration were worked over ten years ago; no fools are left who try." Equally dead: an MDM saving $500k/yr gross but $100k net against a $3M investment — 30-year payback. "You must recompute the revenue side, not polish the savings side." (course day 6, transcript; slides p.16-18)

## Decision points
1. **Do we need a formal DG program at all?** Three honest degrees — "not at all" (no maturity or drivers), "partially", "full-scale". Screen with the 12-statement test (5+ of 12 → likely pays off) and the pre-start question "is the ABSENCE of built-out DG a *current constraint*?" If domains never need each other's data, natural governance suffices — cross-functionality is the trigger word. (course day 1, slides p.103; day 2, slides p.23-24)
2. **Common Sense DG or an MVP?** Run the MVP anyway: "it makes sense not only when approval is difficult — the MVP lets you knock ideas against reality, get a base of concrete themes for scaling, and accelerate the arrival of benefit." Target 3-6 months to visible effect. (course day 6, slides p.104)
3. **Which domain first?** The most critical business domain or a single significant data object (client, finance) — but if no critical domain has a proactive owner, take one of the 2-3 proactive ones and let success spread. (course day 6, slides p.100; day 3, slides p.88)
4. **Go to business now or later?** Later. Start endogenously on platform resources; premature involvement makes business your blocker. Bring them in when you have a packaged MVP result. (course day 6, transcript)
5. **Do we ask for a budget at all?** Conditional. In a money-scrutinizing company a funded DG team inherits an unpayable proof burden — better to "hide governance inside teams with a clearer raison d'être." (course day 5, transcript)
6. **Budget denied — restart or absorb?** Absorb, then restart. Keep chaining initiatives so they pull each other; re-run the MVP when a driver or sponsor changes. Accept it is "much slower than with a focused resource." (course day 6, slides p.100; transcript)
7. **When does the catalog stream start?** When cumulative power-user efficiency loss exceeds the cost of the solution (a U-curve over data-team size, measurable via Time to Value and experiments) *and* adjacent maturity can unlock its value. For 90% of companies, buy rather than build. (course day 2, slides p.35; day 4, slides p.42-44; transcript)
8. **Certify marts, reports or metrics first?** Marts, then reports, then metrics — "even though reports feel closer." And beware the word: "certification smells of bureaucracy to some audiences; consider another term." (course day 3, transcript)
9. **Who leads when there is no DG office?** The Core BI / cross-functional analytics team — metric tree and glossary, criticality classification, golden layer, content management, and data curator in weak domains. Someone must be the visionary or "the teams will hardly coordinate the pieces by themselves." (course day 6, slides p.105; day 5, transcript)
10. **Expand or hold?** Expand on the board's gate — 2 of 4 C-level sponsors, plus the MVP domain owner, plus interest from other domain owners. Below that, keep landing. (course day 6, slides p.100)
11. **What do we stop doing?** Stage 5 makes this an explicit roadmap activity — "abandon ineffective practices, analyse capacity." And the strategic version: "no more ambition that with a billion nobody will give us we'll manage all data to perfection. Don't boil the ocean" — ~80% coverage of critical data is the target, "and I don't need 100%." (board, Program Map 3.0; course day 6, slides p.113-115; transcript)

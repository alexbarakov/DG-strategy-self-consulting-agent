# DG Kitchen of the Tech Industry (2024 research)

## What the board teaches
"Data Governance Kitchen of the Tech Industry" (Alex Barakov, 2024) is an interview study of 20 anonymous tech companies (5 countries, 3,000-100,000+ employees, all with advanced data platforms — incl. a top-5 neobank, a top-3 classified, top-10 delivery and e-commerce platforms), aiming to capture real DG practice "free from marketing". Headline picture: DG in tech is still semi-formal — 40% run no separate DG program (governance is embedded in data-platform processes), programs get justified via a catalog + DQ framework rather than "pure DG", and the final go-decision is usually "a leap of faith" by the data platform head since nobody tracks TTM effects. Top drivers are analyst-facing (data discovery, request TTM), not compliance. Roles adopt asymmetrically: Data Custodian works (65%), Business Data Steward mostly doesn't, committees are absent. DataHub dominates catalogs (~70%), but the author flags "catalog as a shield" — platform teams hiding behind tooling to avoid harder governance work. Glossaries are the biggest gap (2/20), DQ suffers from "tools without processes" (only 10% monitor incident SLAs), and 30% of companies are scaling DG back after prolonged effort without visible results. The research concludes with the author's playbook — Common Sense DG quick wins plus a DG MVP led by a respected internal veteran, expanding land-and-expand style only when sponsorship materializes.

## Key objects
- Methodology: 20 companies, 5 countries, anonymity for honesty; explicit critical-thinking caveat (described approaches may be immaturity, not best practice)
- 8 interview topics: DG start and sponsorship; pains and drivers; role model; data catalog; business glossary; DQ management; efficiency; challenges/plans
- Start & sponsorship: 40% no separate DG program; 45% have a CDO (top-down easiest); 30% bottom-up via infosec; both directions needed; reporting distrust as the "pure DG" trigger
- Ranked drivers: 1 data discovery for analysts; 2 TTM of data requests; 3 quality of critical data; 4 consistent reporting / metric discrepancies; 5 cross-functional data use; 6 compliance risks; 7 M&A data consolidation
- Role model: Data Custodian (aka domain curator / data partner) 65%, filled by systems analysts / data engineers; Data Object Owner rare (compensated by custodian or duty rotation); Business Data Steward hardest; DG committees largely absent (author: dedicated DG meetings are essential)
- Catalog: DataHub ~70%; selection criteria — push approach + contributor community; criticisms — "catalog as a shield"; chat-based data management works only in isolated domains
- Glossary: 2/20 with consolidated glossary + metrics tree; prerequisites — steward skills, cross-domain facilitation, parallel semantic-layer project; author: physical layer / metric store / golden layer more critical
- DQ: 3-level model (core platform contracts+ingestion checks / data engineers transformation checks / BI final cleaning); tooling — Airflow + Great Expectations, dbt test, custom, Soda, Ataccama, Monte Carlo, YAML-driven contracts; problem — tools without processes; 10% monitor DQ SLAs
- Efficiency: 20% cost saving on a 4 PB Hadoop cluster via deletion; catalog ROI proven by chat-search vs catalog-search comparison; 30% scaling DG back; fast benefits correlate with respected senior MVP leads
- Next-year plans: access-as-code, data contracts framework, dbt testing, object/domain ownership, catalog and platform courses
- Author's conclusions: DG = organizational and tooling coordination layer; informal "DG common sense" breaks at scale → formal DG MVP; Core BI / cross-functional analytics team as DG backbone; Custodian/Partner/Curator > Steward in foundational importance; DG leader must be a respected internal veteran (external hires face sabotage risk)

## From the course (Data Governance Fundamentals, 6 days)

Note: on the course this peer material was presented with company names; here it is aggregated, matching the board's anonymity convention.

### How the research was actually conducted
- Its origin is peer-to-peer, not survey-industrial: "I did a round some time back and asked the guys what's going on with them." Leaders talking to leaders under anonymity, which is exactly why the answers include things nobody puts in a conference talk. (course day 2, transcript)
- The eight interview topics map one-to-one onto the board's frames, so the study doubles as a diagnostic template — you can run the same eight conversations inside your own company and get a comparable picture. (board frames; course day 1, slides p.117)
- The disclaimer frame is load-bearing and he repeats it in class: what you are reading may be immaturity rather than best practice. The research does not certify anyone; it records what people admitted. (board frame "Disclaimer")
- Sample bias is stated by the author, not discovered by a reader. On the peer slides: "unfortunately the examples here will be tech ones". On the glossary block: "there's a bias in that, as a rule, all these companies have similar problems — and first of all those problems are not in the glossary area." Read the study as *big-tech* DG, not as DG. (course day 2 and day 5, transcript)
- Sub-samples differ per topic. The catalog and DQ boards carry seven big-tech peers, not all twenty — so "DataHub in two of seven" is the honest denominator on that slide, and the ~70% headline on the board covers a different cut. (course day 4, slides p.87, 145; transcript)
- He does not oversell his own data. Presenting the catalog board he says outright there is "no extra information here" beyond one number that stands out — owners on 30% of objects with an audience of 30 analysts, "a rather dreary current situation". (course day 4, transcript)
- The study keeps getting patched live. Mid-session a participant supplied a follow-up on a peer's second catalog attempt with a commercial vendor; his read was immediate and characteristic — "if they found drivers for a second run, they accumulated investment reasons". (course day 4, transcript)
- The framing question was printed on the summary slide from the start, which is unusual for research authored by a practitioner selling the discipline: "Maybe DG is the case where methodological complexity does more harm than good?" (course day 1, slides p.117)

### Per-topic status across the surveyed big-tech peers
- **Start and sponsorship** — the go-decision is repeatedly a will decision, not a case. At one big-tech player "the catalog was a leap of faith — simply the will of the platform head"; the declared goal was "simplifying analysts' lives", the sponsor was a business CEO who used to be chief analyst, and there was no CDO. Where a CDO did exist, sponsorship was visibly easier — one delivery-tech player summed it up as "we got lucky: a CDO arrived and sponsored it top-down, no need to persuade business". (course day 2, slides p.54; transcript)
- **Catalogs** — one bought a commercial catalog (an open push API was the deciding factor) and rolled it out in under 6 months with 3 part-time people; one tested a popular open-source catalog and dropped it (docs live in the wiki and turn into tech debt); two built their own products, one of them now revising its "the catalog is not for input" concept; one custom build on Atlas has owners on only 30% of objects with an audience of ~30 analysts; one refused vendor lock and built custom on open source — MVP in a quarter with 3 engineers. One of the companies that failed the first attempt has since engaged a vendor for a second run. (course day 4, slides p.87; transcript)
- **Data quality** — one avoids blanket checks on principle: "every check = money" on pay-as-you-go compute, with anomaly self-detection instead; one runs alerts plus cross-checks on golden marts for the most-asked metrics across six domain teams; one runs its own DQ product "voluntarily, without control or goals" (flagged red on the slide); one covers ~10% of critical objects with an Airflow plus home-grown expectations library; one ships a two-check module — "the tool exists = the process doesn't" — while building automatic criticality classification with a 2-day SLA for super-critical fix-and-recalc. (course day 4, slides p.145)
- **Glossaries** — "in big techs the glossary simply doesn't take off": one wanted one but needs business capacity; one never built it (a new attempt is riding on a metric-store project) for lack of domain involvement; one keeps it out of focus; one "sort of" maintains it with domain analysts contributing; one launched a glossary inside a single business line, disconnected from the rest of the metadata and only now being integrated with catalog and semantic layer. Punchline: everyone names metric divergence as almost their main pain, yet nobody builds glossaries — because a glossary needs facilitated cross-domain work with business experts plus a synchronous semantic-layer project. (course day 5, slides p.28; transcript)
- **Roles and culture** — one distributed engineers into domains and still failed to make data partners proactive (they stayed reactive); one has "no calm period for DG projects" thanks to quarterly transformations; in one the culture rejects "regulating rules and imposing roles" outright, so de-facto role acceptance never started and "plans are still in the fog"; one is trying to build ownership across tools inherited from different companies that now need syncing; a delivery-tech player runs everything through code (data-access rules in GitHub, contracts planned, maybe dbt tests) plus internal catalog courses. (course day 6, slides p.107)
- **Efficiency and plans** — the reported wins cluster in two places: storage deletion (enough removed from a 4 PB Hadoop cluster to save 20% of its cost) and search time. Next-year plans across the set are strikingly uniform: access-as-code, a data contracts framework, dbt-style testing, object and domain ownership, and internal courses about the catalog and the platform. (course day 6, slides p.34; board frame "8. Challenges / Plans")

### Role patterns worth stealing
- One big-tech player splits what most companies mash into a single role: a "BI governor" owns only certified reports and reporting standards, while Data Partners (system analysts) own core layers, marts, their certification and optimization. (course day 3, slides p.63)
- One large bank formally has no BI function at all — fully decentralized; a 50+ person cross-data team (templates, standards, courses, tools) informally acts as a BI CoE, at roughly 1 BI developer per 133 users and 10,000 MAU on the BI tool, with a per-domain "CDO" doing domain data strategy while actual governance is done by business data engineers and their team leads. (course day 3, slides p.62; transcript)
- Another large bank hired dedicated business Data Stewards straight from the market as full positions (strategy + DQ processes + project portfolio inside their block) — rare and expensive, but it greatly accelerates policy rollout. (course day 3, slides p.52-53)
- One large telecom ecosystem shows the failure shape of the same ambition: the DG team was elevated to ecosystem level with ~20 stewards, then "dissolved organizationally — it ate a lot of money, internal centres of power over data emerged and competed for budgets; DG practice, methodology and the data platform reported to three different VPs; some parts got terminated." Its salvageable pattern: a DG core split by expertise (catalog lead, metadata lead, reference-data lead), each expert supporting many domain custodians — "the bottom level everyone does everything; the middle level is split into cubes." (course day 1, transcript)

### Effectiveness evidence and war stories
- The efficiency slide states the finding without decoration: "assessments are rather emotional. There are almost no examples of comprehensive analysis of effectiveness and results — everyone is at an earlier stage." (course day 6, slides p.34)
- Concretely: data partners plus a catalog "gave positive vibes", with the TTM effect impossible to assess "because nobody keeps Jira tracking properly — only subjective feedback"; another reports simply that "entropy is decreasing" regarding data, with a plan to eventually look at efficiency, profitability and lifecycle security; a third deleted a lot of data and saved 20% on a 4 PB Hadoop cluster, adding "there hasn't been an effectiveness analysis yet — maybe we'll do one next year". (course day 6, slides p.34)
- Only one company in the survey ran an actual analysis — chat search time without a catalog vs catalog search statistics, number of requests × analyst FTE cost — and its results green-lit further investment. One out of twenty. (course day 6, slides p.34; transcript)
- One peer instrumented the warehouse itself rather than the program: a DWH-for-DWH usage dashboard with per-layer object usage, naming compliance, unfilled descriptions by domain, and metrics handed to domain data partners — share of certified objects, share of queries landing in the certified layer, % legacy objects, DQ incident resolution speed, number of requests arriving in support chats. That is the closest thing in the whole study to a governance control panel. (course day 6, slides p.32)
- Big techs never present DG at conferences as a revenue driver — governance there is dissolved into platform processes, "it runs by default"; external catalogs are unsellable to big tech, only mid/small tech buys. (course day 6, transcript)
- A large fintech recently cut its entire DG team — stewards and catalog support ("they'll probably resuscitate it later"). Standing lesson: in the fight for engineers between platform product streams, the catalog loses; DG lives in a structurally weak position — which is why the author refuses to build DG around a negative vibe and pushes "common sense DG." (course day 2, transcript)
- The counter-example the study keeps returning to sits outside tech: a mid-size company where a DG-as-a-service team of 2 FTE spent a quarter investigating data and reports for named executive pains ("I can't trust reports", late invoices), then produced a day-by-day breakdown of the month-end closing process showing 2-3 excess workdays burned by 11 finance employees and invoice deltas from 100€ to over 100k€ — confirmed by the Head of Internal Accounting, taken to the committee with an internal auditor as witness. A rare genuine DG business case, and it exists precisely where the platform was *not* mature. (course day 2, slides p.41-43; course day 6, slides p.108-110)
- The research's framing question, stated explicitly on the course: "Maybe DG is the case where methodological complexity does more harm than good?" (course day 1, slides p.117)

## Patterns that repeat
- **Everyone has a catalog project; nobody has a permanent catalog team.** Catalogs are chronically under-invested — even when the project launches, almost nobody justifies a standing team, so every catalog shows a "fading trend". Open-source builds specifically get abandoned half-finished because competing initiatives always win the engineers. (course day 2, transcript)
- **The technical custodian role exists everywhere, under a local name.** Data partner, domain curator, BI partner, business data engineer — the same person, arrived at independently by companies that never compared notes. In ~95% of cases it is a second hat, not a position. (course day 1 and day 3, transcript)
- **The business data steward is designed everywhere and staffed almost nowhere.** Business people will answer questions, approve definitions and state DQ expectations; they will not do hands-on stewardship in any workflow you design. Where the role is stapled onto business-function duties, disengagement is the standard outcome. (course day 3, transcript; slides p.78, 82)
- **Governance dissolves into the platform.** In tech the honest description of DG is not a program but a set of platform processes with named drivers, which is also why it never appears in conference decks as a revenue story. 40% of the sample runs no separate program at all. (course day 6, transcript; board frame 1)
- **Compliance is not the engine.** Ranked drivers put analyst discovery and request TTM first and compliance sixth — the inverse of the ordering every vendor deck assumes. (board frame 2)
- **Nobody measures, and everybody knows it.** No Jira discipline, no baseline, no attribution; effectiveness lives as "positive vibes" and "entropy is decreasing". Where money *was* computed, it was computed once, for one use case, and it was enough. (course day 6, slides p.34)
- **The glossary is universally named as the answer to the universally named pain — and universally not built.** 2 of 20. Metric divergence tops the pain list in the same interviews. (board frames 2 and 5; course day 5, slides p.28)
- **Tools arrive before processes.** "The tool exists = the process doesn't" is the recurring shape: a DQ module with two checks, a DQ product used "voluntarily, without control or goals", a catalog in prod with view-only input and ~10% adoption, checker coverage at 10% of critical objects. Only 10% of the sample monitors DQ incident SLAs. (course day 4, slides p.145; board frame 6)
- **Committees do not appear.** Not because tech is enlightened but because tech culture rejects them; the coordination that does happen runs under other names or not at all. (board frame 3; course day 5, transcript)
- **Deletion is the one saving everyone can prove.** Infrastructure freed by removing redundant data is the only *direct* DG economy in the whole study; everything else is indirect to some degree. (course day 6, slides p.34; course day 2, transcript)
- **The culture objection is the same sentence in different companies.** Imposed rules, distributed roles and routine rituals are considered bullshit; "regulating rules and imposing roles gets rejected"; DG "conflicts with tech-company culture". (course day 1, slides p.117; course day 6, slides p.99, 107)
- **And they survive anyway.** "Most leading companies live in chaos while doing isolated sensible things — 'common sense DG'. They live and they don't break." That is the study's single most-quoted finding, and it is the one that undercuts the discipline's own sales pitch. (course day 6, slides p.99)

## What only worked where…
- **…the catalog buy was a decision, not a deliberation.** The fastest rollout in the set — under 6 months, 3 part-time people — happened at the company that picked a commercial product on one decisive criterion (an open push API) instead of comparing everything. The slowest outcomes are the custom builds and the abandoned pilot. (course day 4, slides p.87)
- **…the vendor lock objection was backed by a real team.** Refusing vendors worked in exactly one place, and only because it funded an MVP in a quarter with three engineers and then a team of up to ten in year two. Without that headcount, the same decision produces an ugly metadata store. (course day 4, slides p.87; transcript)
- **…a gate sat in the delivery flow.** "No description — no prod deploy" moved table-description quality from 49% to 86% in a year across ~7000 tables. Nothing voluntary in the study moved a number like that. Softener that made it survivable: an AI "generate description" button next to the gate. (course day 4, slides p.48; transcript)
- **…someone paid for a dedicated steward.** The bank that hired business Data Stewards from the market as full positions is the only place where policy rollout visibly accelerated — rare, expensive, and the honest counter-evidence to "second hat is enough". (course day 3, slides p.52-53)
- **…the role was split rather than merged.** Separating a reporting-standards owner from mart-and-core-layer owners produced clarity that the single-steward companies never reached. (course day 3, slides p.63)
- **…the pain belonged to a named executive with a number attached.** The genuine business case in the study came from a CFO-level complaint traced to 2-3 excess workdays × 11 finance employees and invoice deltas up to six figures — and it funded a 3-person team off an Excel of small monetized cases. (course day 2, slides p.41-43)
- **…the platform measured itself.** The one peer running a DWH-for-DWH dashboard with per-partner certification and certified-layer query share is the one that can answer "is it getting better?" without adjectives. (course day 6, slides p.32)
- **…the leader was an internal veteran.** Fast benefits correlate with a respected senior lead running the MVP. Hire from the market and "you'll wait years for him to become one — nobody lets a newcomer seriously change their processes; you get sabotage and imitation." (board frame "success factors"; course day 6, slides p.104)
- **…there was a calm period.** The company transforming its processes every quarter never got a DG project off the ground; it is the clearest single-variable explanation in the whole set. (course day 6, slides p.107)

## Honest admissions worth preserving verbatim-ish
- "We have no calm period for DG-type projects; everyone works inside their own domain and doesn't go outside — there's no access there anyway, so chats are enough for us, we don't need a catalog." (course day 2, slides p.54; transcript)
- On a home-grown DQ product: "used voluntarily, without control or goals" — flagged red by the author on his own slide. (course day 4, slides p.145)
- "The tool exists = the process doesn't." (course day 4, slides p.145)
- "There hasn't been an effectiveness analysis yet — maybe we'll do one next year." (course day 6, slides p.34)
- "After distributing engineers into domains we failed to get a proactive approach from data partners. They continued to act reactively." (course day 6, slides p.107)
- "Regulating rules and imposing roles gets rejected. Awareness that data is not only yours comes slowly. De-facto acceptance of roles hasn't happened, nobody has even started. Plans are still in the fog." (course day 6, slides p.107)
- Documentation "lives in the wiki and lands as tech debt". (course day 4, slides p.87)
- "Every check = money." (course day 4, slides p.145)
- The author about his own company, on the same footing as the peers: "we say we have an à-la-data-mesh architecture, but of the four components we really only have one — the platform. No self-service, no proper domain structure, no federated governance." And: "immediately maximum Wild West — full decentralization, freedom, and governance activities never took root; we're still cleaning that up." (course day 1 and day 2, transcript)
- Also his own: DQ checkers hang directly on core-layer tables and columns, skipping the business-term and business-rule layer entirely — "a consequence of a high degree of freedom and a poor level of governance", so you cannot see which business rules exist for a field. (course day 5, transcript)
- And the admission he hesitated to publish: an honest calculation of his whole DG slice produced a monthly money effect that looked very small against the scale of his company — small enough that he weighed whether to show the number at all. The lesson travels without the figure: a truthful DG ROI rarely looks impressive next to product economics.

## What the author concludes for practitioners
- **DG is an overlay, and that is the whole point.** "Data Governance is just an organizational superstructure plus tooling that focuses and coordinates resources so things actually get done and can live, instead of dangling forever in separate teams' roadmaps." If your reaction to the research is "we do this anyway without DG" — great, that *is* the answer. (course day 6, slides p.101)
- **Start with common-sense DG and let it tear.** Standardized data-lifecycle operations (Data/BIOps) + basic DQ checks and tooling + DWH/BI metrics reporting already delivers significant value through existing teams. At platform scale it tears — you need a role model beyond your own teams, scaled DQ, data classification, cross-team coordination — and DWH/BI leads lack the authority, interest and resource to assemble the whole puzzle. That is the moment for a DG MVP. Or for not starting, if drivers haven't reached critical mass. (course day 6, slides p.102)
- **Run an MVP even when approval is easy.** It lets you knock ideas against reality, builds a base of concrete scaling themes and accelerates time-to-value. Dashboard certification shipped in three months plus a data cleanup event worked on the metrics *and* emotionally — buying licence for more expensive initiatives. (course day 6, slides p.104; transcript)
- **Custodian over Steward.** "The Data Custodian / Data Partner / Data Curator role is probably more critical than Data Steward — and at minimum it comes first." It distributes across the data platform and domain data roles and covers the whole operational cycle. (course day 6, slides p.105)
- **A cross-functional Core BI team is the backbone.** Critical for the metric tree and glossary, object criticality classification, the golden layer and content management — and it takes the curator role in "weak" domains. (course day 6, slides p.105)
- **The leader decides more than the design.** DG must be reached by data and analytics leaders themselves — "it is useless to explain DG to the business and wait for enthusiastic support; business will support you but will never postpone its own tasks for DG tickets". The maturity of your own team in key positions decides, and the leader must be an authoritative old-timer. (course day 6, slides p.104)
- **Align priorities before publishing approaches.** All key DG initiatives are cross-role; "'we developed an approach but they don't follow it' — kindergarten". The DG leader's job is mediation. (course day 6, slides p.105)
- **Don't build the program on a negative vibe.** Given how structurally weak DG's position is — the catalog loses the fight for engineers, whole DG teams get cut — a program sold as "everything is broken" is fragile by construction. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Certification is the practical substitute for a program.** Marts, then reports, then metrics, then semantic-layer objects, then agents — the chain the author actually built, without a DG team, "past the point of no return". It is also the AI prerequisite that now defends the budget. (course day 5 and day 6, transcript)
- **Aim for ~80% of critical data, not 100%.** "You can't properly manage even critical data to 100%, and I don't need 100%." (course day 6, transcript)
- **Measure something, once, honestly.** One company in twenty computed a real number and it green-lit investment. Be that company — and expect the number to be modest, because you are multiplying minutes by headcount. (course day 6, slides p.34; course day 4, transcript)

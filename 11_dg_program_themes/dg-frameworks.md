# Data Governance Frameworks

## What the board teaches
A DG framework is defined (per the Data Governance Institute) as "a logical structure for classifying, organizing, and communicating the complex activities associated with enterprise data decision-making and action-taking" — the conceptual blueprint of a program, explicitly *not yet* an implementation project. The board surveys seven reference models — DGI's ten-component model, PwC's four-layer Enterprise Data Governance Framework, the DAMA Wheel and DAMA Pyramid (Peter Aiken), the Eckerson Group's six-layer people-centric framework, Informatica's, and Seiner's Non-Invasive Data Governance — and then refuses to pick one. The recommended recipe is: (1) study several existing frameworks, (2) draft your focus areas — core elements, what hurts *you* most, and explicit exclusions, (3) sketch your own with the team in a half-day session. The "Your DG Framework" mind map shows what a company-level framework must define: from key terms and program goals through role model, cost-benefit model, policy, tech stack and communications — with DG Vision as input and Funding/Executive Support as the enabling condition.

## Key objects
- DG framework definition (DGI quote); caveat "abstract model, not an implementation project"
- DGI Data Governance Framework: 10 universal components (why-what-who-how) grouped into rules, people, processes; Goals/Metrics/Funding; preventive/corrective Controls; Data Governance Office (DGO)
- PwC Enterprise Data Governance Framework (from "Demystifying Data Governance", Nov 2019): Data Management; Enablers (People & Culture, Process & Operating Model, Tools & Technology); Stewardship & Core Layer (lineage, glossary, master data, metadata, quality & trust, access & ownership, privacy & security); Governance Strategy & Vision (Control/Efficiency vs Growth/Monetization)
- Other referenced models: DAMA Wheel (DG at the center of knowledge areas), DAMA Pyramid (Aiken), Eckerson Group (6 layers, 39 components), Informatica, Non-Invasive DG (Seiner), Willem Koenders' building analogy; McKinsey and Deloitte listed as examples but used only for the program-type 2×2
- 3-step "build your own framework" recipe
- "Your DG Framework" mind map — 10 branches: key DG terms; program goals (value drivers, pain points, effectiveness metrics); data prioritization (domain classification, access rights matrix); operational management model (roles, governance bodies, monitoring); cost-benefit model; DG processes and responsibility matrix; high-level DG policy; priority initiatives; DG technology stack (catalog, DQ tool); communication and marketing system
- Root chain: DG Vision → Your DG Framework → Funding and Executive Support
- A dedicated DG framework workshop template exists — see [templates.md](../12_templates/templates.md)

## From the course (Data Governance Fundamentals, 6 days)

### What a framework is actually for
- The deck mocks the framework-as-panacea posture *before* it defines one: the definition slide carries a Toy Story meme, "JUST USE THIS — IT CAN SOLVE ANY PROBLEM". Everything after that is read against the meme. (course day 1, slides p.105)
- The working definition he keeps is short and deliberately modest: a framework is "the how — a blueprint for enforcing governance", "a certain simplified structure that directs the implementation of DG in an organization". Its job is to fold everything into one picture and let you "explain on your fingers to your company what is actually needed". (course day 1, slides p.105, 114)
- Hence the verdict he puts on the whole tour in one line: "the meaning is plus-minus the same in all of them. The question is which is closer to *your* company and better helps you fold everything into a picture." (course day 1, slides p.114)
- Sequencing matters more than choice: he recommends sketching the framework "somewhere at the implementation stage — when you have already understood the problems and drawn out the goals and the organizational model", not as the opening act. The framework encodes conclusions you already reached; it does not generate them. (course day 1, transcript)
- The homework definition is the sharpest one, because exclusion is built into it: "Data Governance framework — structuring the data-management elements relevant for the company into a form practical for implementation, *while excluding all unneeded components*." (course day 1, slides p.122)
- The politics: a framework's real function is scope defence and focus retention over a long horizon, not control. "Maybe a bit of bureaucracy won't hurt us now" — a framework is what makes "boring but systematic movement" trackable across a year. (course day 5, slides p.64)
- Packaging trick that follows from that, from a real HR-domain case at a large tech company: an existing stream of DQ projects was relabelled "DG working group + committee" and sponsors from platform, HR and analytics were invited — solely to make progress reportable upward. "Ordinary work, packaged so it can be tracked." (course day 5, slides p.64; transcript)
- The counter-force is that the framework is an *internal* artefact for data leaders. Marching to business under the literal flag "Data Governance" fails in ~95% of cases: business does not want to understand what DG is, and the word itself reads as "distant and secondary". Better to not even call it DG. (course day 2, transcript)
- And without a DG office you will never get one consolidated program anyway — you assemble it piecemeal (dashboard certification extended into mart certification with the DWH head, split between two teams), and documentation ends up existing per-component, not per-program. "And that's fine." (course day 5, transcript)
- Diagnostic he repeats: "as many companies as I see, that many different DG frameworks" — even unformalized ones can be drawn. If your company already governs data somehow, your framework exists; the exercise is to make it visible, not to import one. (course day 1, transcript)

### The frameworks he actually references, and what he does with each
- **DGI** — the source of the definition he quotes, not of the model he uses. His comment while flipping through it is deliberately flat: "in principle it repeats plus-minus what we said, adds some components; you can read about them separately." What it is genuinely good for is forcing four things onto one page that most home-made pictures omit — decision rights, accountabilities, control mechanisms and funding. What it hides: it presumes a Data Governance Office exists to run the ten components. (course day 1, slides p.106; transcript)
- **DAMA Wheel** — stamped on the slide "bad framework, explains nothing". But the nuance he adds out loud is the reason to keep it at all: the wheel does say one true thing — DG sits in the centre and answers for strategy, roles, policies and standards, issue resolution and valuation, i.e. it is a *coordinating* influence over disciplines that exist with or without it, so that all the surrounding blocks aim at one set of goals with nothing missing and nothing extra. "In general it's fine — probably that is how you should look at it." What it hides is everything operational: sequence, cost, staffing, and permission to skip petals. Read literally it is a bill for eleven programs. (course day 1, slides p.107; transcript)
- **DAMA/Aiken Pyramid** — same stamp, plus a specific objection. The pyramid claims you cannot build a layer without the previous one; "that looks like artificial logic, because we know a heap of examples where all of it happens in a different order, not necessarily such a beautiful one. So we skip this story again — keep it just for information." (course day 1, slides p.108; transcript)
- A participant pushed on the pyramid live: roles don't exist in a vacuum, so where do stewards sit — and if a role is smeared across layers it would need an implausible breadth of competencies. His answer is the useful part: the segmentation has no such logic. Domain stewards originate in the DG block but work in slices of DQ, metadata and architectural oversight *inside their own domain*. Do not staff a framework by reading roles off its layers. (course day 1, transcript)
- **Eckerson Group** — the one he speaks warmly about ("very cool guys, lots of deep articles"), and his stated use is modest and honest: "a decent picture for making a slide for the presentation where you describe what your program will consist of", with the right-hand column of concrete actions per layer as the useful bit. Its thesis is also the one he agrees with most: "the reality is that we don't govern the data. We govern what people do when working with data." Eckerson also supplies two artefacts he reuses elsewhere — the Guides / Guardrails / Gates vocabulary and the DG-platform evaluation criteria spreadsheet. (course day 1, slides p.109; course day 4, slides p.33–34; course day 6, slides p.117)
- **PwC** — "the same eggs, side view. But an organizational level is added, which grounds the abstractions on the company a little." The genuinely usable element is the strategic slider at the top: Control/Efficiency ↔ Growth/Monetization. That is the one thing in any of these pictures a sponsor can actually take a position on. (course day 1, slides p.110)
- **Informatica** — "a very similar framework". Its shape is the vendor shape: business drivers → governance outcomes → people → processes → technology. Worth naming precisely because the chain is built to terminate at tooling; useful as a top-down narrative, not as a scope. (course day 1, slides p.111; transcript)
- **Willem Koenders' building analogy** — not a framework but a translation device, and the board keeps it for a reason. He used it in consulting for a real-estate classifieds company where "data asset = building" was literally the client's world: owner/landlord, steward/property manager, consumers/tenants, data contract/lease agreement, policies and standards/zoning and fire code, DQ/property condition, usage/occupation rate. "You can draw a lot of cool analogies here — adapt this thing for yourself so the associative things are tied to your industry." Steal the mechanism, not the building. (course day 1, slides p.112; transcript)
- **Non-Invasive DG (Seiner)** — present on the board's list of seven, never sold as a brand. It is nevertheless the stance he actually runs (see below), which is why the board lists it and the course never lectures on it.
- **McKinsey** — used for exactly one thing, and not as a framework: the program-type 2×2, industry regulation level × data complexity, where regulation pushes the minimum sophistication bar up and complexity grows with business variety, core-data velocity and *low* automation maturity. (course day 1, slides p.104)

### Build your own: the recipe, the template, and the deletion step
- Three steps, no more: study a few ready frameworks; sketch what must be in focus — core elements, what hurts you most, and "what is better removed for now so it doesn't distract"; then "sit down as a team for half a day and draw". (course day 1, slides p.115)
- The warning printed in red on that slide is the one people ignore: "this is not yet a DG implementation project — it is an abstract mock-up of how it works." (course day 1, slides p.115)
- His own template is introduced with self-irony — "more frameworks for the framework god" — and is 10 core components "numbered in the order of optimal comprehension (not strictly)": DG terms for your company → program goals (drivers, pains, effectiveness metrics) → data prioritization (domain classification + access matrix) → operating model (roles, governing bodies, project monitoring) → cost-benefit model → DG processes and responsibility matrix → top-level DG policy → list of priority initiatives → technology stack → communication and marketing. Capped by DG Vision, underpinned by funding and executive support. (course day 1, slides p.116)
- The deletion step is the method, not a caveat, and it recurs across the whole course: the goals configurator is explicitly subtractive ("the first move is to delete the goals and initiatives not relevant to you"); the maturity canvas starts by greying out the bricks you don't need ("your maturity starts with removing what doesn't apply to you"); the strategy canvas instruction is "delete everything that doesn't fit your company's maturity". A framework assembled without a visible exclusion list is not finished. (course day 2, slides p.57; transcript; course day 6, slides p.124)
- Cost realism belongs inside the framework, not after it: "the cost of individual initiatives can exceed their value at your maturity, size and business specifics — then there is no sense starting them until something changes. It is logical to build from the simple and add complexity as maturity grows." (course day 6, slides p.101)
- **Lean Data Governance (Marcel Dybalski)** is the one imported one-pager he endorses — "of LinkedIn bullshit, this one is good": pick 1 problem statable in one sentence; 1 domain, 3–10 datasets, 1 source of truth per thing; 3 minimum roles (one person may wear many hats); least-access defaults; start with 3 automated checks; one-page docs; weekly 30-min data office hours; quarterly expand to the next 3 datasets. Success = fewer "which number is right?" debates, faster access, quicker onboarding, less dashboard breakage. It is on the board because it is the only framework in the set sized for a company that cannot staff a framework. (course day 1, slides p.56, 119)

### Non-invasive as a stance, not a brand
- With no dedicated budget he runs what he calls invisible / non-invasive DG: chaining initiatives that pull each other — the core layer pulls DQ checks, the checks pull the catalog, the domains pull roles. "It works, but much slower than with a focused resource." (course day 6, transcript)
- Staffing follows recognition rather than appointment: the data-custodian operations list is "recognizable, isn't it — this is the ordinary daily work of our teams, without any extra roles", so the three legitimate moves are appoint / dedicate / *recognize*, and in every domain running on common sense there is already someone instinctively archiving, certifying and documenting. Find them and confer the role. (course day 3, slides p.60, 80; transcript)
- Tom Sawyer as an engagement model, run for real: gamified BI cleanup marathons whose participants are told afterwards that what they did was stewardship. That is how his BI-partner program was bootstrapped. (course day 3, slides p.85; course day 2, transcript)
- Naming hygiene is part of the stance. Don't call it a committee — "data management sync" sounds less bureaucratic and works the same. Beware "certification": it "smells of bureaucracy" to some audiences; consider another term. Localize role names to the industry (a timber company decided its stewards would be "data foresters"). And avoid DAMA/DMBOK/Kimball vocabulary with business — "not because they are wrong, but this language gets in the way", and professional jargon is itself a misalignment source when one person says measures, another metrics, a third indicators. (course day 5, transcript; course day 3, transcript; course day 1, transcript; course day 5, transcript)
- Enforcement is inverted rather than softened: from develop → monitor → enforce, to facilitate policy writing → prevention/warning → intervention → enforcement as a rare last resort reserved for genuinely critical things like personal data. Guides / Guardrails / Gates, where guardrails mean changing the environment so it is hard to do it wrong. Principle on the slide: "govern with the belief that most people want to do the right thing." (course day 6, slides p.116–117)
- Policy minimalism against the standards spiral (each new regulation, data type and self-service wave adds policies → worse navigation → lower understanding and compliance): new policies only when needed, archive when stale, and push policy into tools "so the policy itself becomes unnecessary". (course day 6, slides p.118–119)
- The anti-brand corollary is blunt: "90% of projects never get that budget", so most companies are forced into common-sense DG anyway — and in companies that scrutinize money hard it may be strategically *better* not to form a dedicated DG team at all, because a funded program inherits the burden of proving payback, while governance work hidden inside teams with a clearer raison d'être does not. (course day 5, transcript)

### Governing bodies without the bureaucracy
- Committees, councils and offices are "not a mismatch — it is the standard management system of any enterprise program", nothing DG-specific. But DAMA's full body stack (Steering Committee / DG Council / DG Office / steward teams / local councils) is "a maximum program. Without necessity, build a smaller-scale governance system" — small organizations should explicitly skip local councils. (course day 5, slides p.56, 68)
- Real committees only emerge at transnational scale — "maybe two dozen companies max" in Russia. Below that, the same coordination happens under another name. (course day 5, transcript)
- Whatever you call it, run it honestly: expect stewards to ask "should I keep attending?" after the very first meeting, have a ready answer, repeat it every session, admit it when someone's honest answer is "no", never meet for the meeting's sake, and close by listing what was achieved that day. (course day 3, slides p.86–87)
- Calibration for escalation design (DAMA): 80–85% of data conflicts should be resolved at business-unit stewardship level, under 20% reach the DG Council, under 5% the Steering Committee. If your picture routes more upward than that, the bodies are load-bearing and you will need to staff them. (course day 5, slides p.65)

### Curate, don't govern — and where the AI era stresses the classics
- The rethink slide is a tug-of-war: agility, speed, self-service and self-governance pulling against policy enforcement, complexity and volume, decision-rights hierarchy and strict processes. Every classic framework was drawn from the right-hand side of that rope. (course day 6, slides p.113)
- The label stayed "Data Governance" but the meaning shifted from governing to curating: observing data use instead of controlling it, agility and time-to-value instead of compliance, quality per use-case instead of enterprise-wide, collaboration instead of roles-and-duties, maximizing reuse instead of authorized use. "No more ambition that with a billion nobody will give us we'll manage all data to perfection. Don't boil the ocean." Target ~80% on critical data — "you can't properly manage even critical data to 100%, and I don't need 100%." (course day 6, slides p.113–115; transcript)
- AI amplifies everything on the left of the rope at once: "the feeling is that a dam has been opened. One quarter of chasing efficiency is enough to flood the whole valley. Governance must be thought through. Immediately." (course day 6, slides p.96)
- None of the classic wheels has a cell for what now needs governing: agents and skills as assets requiring catalog entries, ownership and certification; artefact volume growing by an order of magnitude; agent identity distinct from user identity ("today agent access = user access"); observability of agent actions; token economics — "after the Wild West strategy comes the counting of burned tokens". (course day 6, slides p.93, 96; transcript)
- LLMs also collapsed the cost of the one deliverable classic frameworks optimize for: "writing and updating policies became simple — detailed accents in the prompt and compile. What's left is to find those who will read them." Document *production* is no longer the constraint; document readability and relevance are. Same for the framework-drafting consulting task itself — "any LLM will draft a better typical domain structure than I will". (course day 5, slides p.93; transcript; course day 3, transcript)
- And AI supplies the framework's new political defence. In cost cuts there is no dedicated DG FTE to cut because DG is smeared across teams; the defence runs through AI-readiness — "under all our agents there must be a core data layer, a semantic layer and certified reports — and certification can only be achieved communally." (course day 3, transcript)
- The honest coda: he seriously considered rebuilding the entire course, because AI is invalidating chunks of DG practice in real time. Treat any framework picture, including his own, as having a shorter shelf life than the program it describes. (course day 1, transcript)

## Anti-patterns
- **Framework theatre.** Presenting the abstract mock-up as if it were a plan. The slide says it in red — "this is not yet a DG implementation project" — and it is the most-ignored line in the deck. (course day 1, slides p.115)
- **Adopting a wheel you cannot staff.** DAMA's full body stack is "a maximum program"; local councils in a small org, or a per-domain pack of owner + business steward + technical steward when nobody has 0.2 FTE to give, produces an org chart with no people in it. Every box needs a name, a team and a percentage of someone's week. (course day 5, slides p.56; course day 1 and day 5, transcript)
- **Importing a framework without the deletion step.** Copying eleven petals because they are printed means committing to eleven programs. If you cannot say aloud what you removed and why, you have not built a framework — you have photocopied one. (course day 1, slides p.115, 122; course day 2, slides p.57)
- **Drawing the framework before the pains.** It belongs after problem analysis and goal-setting; drawn first it becomes an aesthetic object that nobody can trace back to a business complaint. (course day 1, transcript)
- **Phase dogma.** "You cannot build layer N without layer N-1" is artificial; real companies do it in any order. (course day 1, slides p.108)
- **Marching under the DG flag.** ~95% failure rate with business; the word evokes "distant and secondary". Package it under the pain it solves. (course day 2, transcript)
- **Speaking DMBOK to business.** The vocabulary itself is a misalignment source, and it signals academic exercise exactly where you need to sound like operations. (course day 5, transcript)
- **Announcing a committee.** Same meeting, worse reception. "Data management sync" works identically. (course day 5, transcript)
- **The standards spiral.** More policies → worse navigation → lower understanding and compliance. Minimalism plus archiving plus pushing rules into tools. (course day 6, slides p.118–119)
- **Treating beautiful documentation as the deliverable.** Documentation decay is structural, not a discipline failure — his own BI strategy went stale between writing and socialization. Current, beautiful DG documentation exists only where a DG office is paid to maintain it: conservative, long-cycle companies. "Quite an expensive thing if you want it beautiful and current." (course day 5, transcript)
- **"We developed an approach but they don't follow it."** Named on the success-factors slide as "kindergarten". All key DG initiatives are cross-role; the framework's owner is the mediator, not the author of rules others must discover. (course day 6, slides p.105; transcript)
- **Standing up a funded DG team in a money-scrutinizing company.** You inherit the payback burden along with the budget. Sometimes the stronger play is to leave governance hidden inside teams that already justify themselves. (course day 5, transcript)

## Questions to ask when choosing (or refusing) a framework
**Before you pick anything**
- Is the *absence* of built-out DG a current constraint for the company — or are there more pressing problems and the chaos simply isn't big enough to hurt yet? (course day 2, slides p.23)
- Do 5 or more of the 12 DG-need statements apply to you? (course day 2, slides p.24)
- Where do you sit on regulation × data complexity, and what program archetype does your domains × business-processes count imply — master data, financial reporting, or the full corporate KPI/DQ/strategy set? (course day 1, slides p.103–104)
- Can you draw the unformalized framework you already run? Which components exist and simply have no name? (course day 1, transcript)

**About the picture in front of you**
- Does it name what hurts *you* most, or what hurt the framework author's clients? (course day 1, slides p.115)
- What are you deleting from it, and can you defend each deletion out loud? (course day 1, slides p.122)
- For every role in it: who is this person on Monday, in which team, at what share of their time, and who signs that off? (course day 1 and day 5, transcript)
- Does it survive being executed out of order — or does it assume phases? (course day 1, slides p.108)
- What does each component cost at your current maturity, and which ones cost more than they are worth today? (course day 6, slides p.101)
- Who reads the documents this framework asks you to produce? If nobody, the framework is asking for the wrong artefacts. (course day 5, slides p.93; transcript)
- Where do agents live in this picture — as assets, as actors, and as a cost line? (course day 6, slides p.96; transcript)

**About what you actually need from it**
- Is your framework's function control, or focus retention across a year? (course day 5, slides p.64)
- Would relabelling an existing stream and inviting sponsors give you 80% of the benefit without a program? (course day 5, slides p.64)
- Would your sponsor recognize their own pain in this picture — and would you dare show it to them without saying the words "data governance"? (course day 2, transcript)
- If you cannot staff a framework at all, is Lean DG's one-problem/one-domain/three-roles/three-checks shape the honest answer? (course day 1, slides p.119)

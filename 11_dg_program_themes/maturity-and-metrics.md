# Maturity and Metrics

## What the board teaches
Two instruments answer "where are we and is it working". The Data & Analytics Maturity Map is a self-assessment canvas of 7 dimensions and 25 sub-areas, where capability blocks are laid out along a progression from *Data informed* → *Data Driven* → *Data Led* and color-coded by status (done / done-but-improvable / in progress / not started / not applicable). Three columns run down the right edge — business value of change, cost of change, priority — so the map doubles as a prioritisation canvas rather than a scorecard, and its footnote is explicit: the coloured gap between current and target state is what the value of your initiatives is measured against. Its worked progressions are the most reusable part — e.g. reporting evolves from on-request reports through central BI and federated CoE to GenAI BI; DQ from custom checks through monitoring tools and criticality coverage to data contracts and observability; roles engagement from "natural chaotic DG with no driver" through data-people stewardship and business engagement to a federated DG model "dissolved in processes". The Data-Driven Index then turns maturity into a single weighted number: DD Index = Σ(weights × component) over 13 components and 26 example metrics with weights summing to 1.00 (worked example: 62.99), explicitly to be tailored to what a company can actually calculate and what is meaningful for its business. Reference industry models (CMMI's DMM, EDM Council's DCAM, plus comparisons) are catalogued separately for calibration.

## Key objects
- Maturity map dimensions: 1 Getting value from data; 2 Analytics services (reporting factory, self-serve, experiments, advanced analytics); 3 Analytics Governance (content management, customer development, knowing the data); 4 Data Platform Architecture (storage, ingestion, transformation, BI, ML/DS/AI); 5 Data Governance (discovery & cataloging, DQ system, security, roles engagement); 6 Data Team (people management, roles, structure, project management); 7 Culture (executive leadership, decision-making, data culture)
- Maturity stages: Data informed → Data Driven → Data Led; status legend (6 statuses); per-row business value / cost / priority columns; caveat that block order isn't always a level-up
- Reference models: DMM (CMMI Institute, link now 404), DCAM (EDM Council), model comparison cluster, other models
- Data-Driven Index formula: DD Index = Σ(weights × Component); 26-row grid (component / metric example / complexity / formula / value / weight / contribution)
- 13 DDI components with example weights: Data Literacy 0.15, Tools Adoption 0.15, Direct Business Value 0.13, Data Management 0.12, Data Accessibility 0.12, Organisational Model 0.09, Culture 0.06, Analytics Maturity 0.03, Innovation 0.03, Customer Insights 0.03, Data Security 0.03, Data Infrastructure 0.03, Leadership 0.03
- Worked example: total 62.99; weakest metrics NPS improvement (10%), cost reduction from data decisions (14%); strongest infrastructure uptime (99%), ad-hoc duration (95%)
- DDI dashboard mockup: index card 62.9 (+11.1 YoY); note that formula and component list are company-specific
- Data Literacy component detail: role skill profiles with L1-L4 targets, individual and team scores (see [data-literacy.md](data-literacy.md))
- Both instruments are working templates — see [templates.md](../12_templates/templates.md)

## From the course (Data Governance Fundamentals, 6 days)

### Measuring a DG program honestly
- The opening position is a concession, not a pitch: "financial ROI from quality data is hard to measure." You can measure concrete things, but "it will almost never be the sexy number for which you'd be handed a real budget" — and the total, after a great deal of effort, "will most likely be fairly modest. That's the reality." (course day 6, slides p.4; transcript)
- Which means the honest framing of what a DG case *is*: "as a rule, all such proofs work on moral authority or on emotional argumentation." You describe what will not happen if this isn't done, and which initiatives stay blocked by your current level of data-management maturity. That is a legitimate argument — but call it what it is before the CFO does. (course day 6, transcript)
- The slide's own instruction for building the case is three verbs, none of them "calculate": work *with business-process owners* to form a base of benefits from higher data quality; assemble an ROI model that matches business benefit against progress and cost; and develop and *accumulate* DG business cases. Maturity models and scorecards are listed on the same slide as the alternative routes when the money route stalls. (course day 6, slides p.4)
- Definition worth keeping because it forecloses the argument: "value is the difference between the cost of creating or acquiring a thing and the benefit received from it. There are no standard methodologies for calculating the value of data." (course day 6, slides p.9)
- Where the honest number lands: an honest calculation of the author's whole DG slice produced a monthly money effect that looked "very little" against the scale of his company — small enough that he hesitated to show it at all. Expect the same. A truthful DG ROI rarely looks impressive next to product economics, and that is a property of the domain, not of your arithmetic. (course day 6, transcript)

### Three real sources of ROI, and three that are air
- Only three zones are worth searching: revenue growth via linking DG initiatives to business initiatives and business cases; cost savings; mitigation of regulator-related risk. (course day 6, slides p.4-5; transcript)
- On the classic six-petal "ROI of Data Governance" wheel he crosses two out by name. *Operational efficiency* — "we get access to reliable data faster, we shorten time-to-insight" — is "a very shaky thing… in general it's air." *Innovation* — faster adoption, faster adaptation to change — "also shaky." (course day 6, slides p.5; transcript)
- *Data quality* is on the wheel as a separate petal and he refuses it: "in my view it isn't really an independent area" — reducing incidents or their resolution time always resolves into revenue or cost, and you should draw that line yourself instead of leaving DQ as its own claim. (course day 6, slides p.5; transcript)
- The whole indirect-benefit list gets a red X and a castle-in-the-clouds picture: multiplier on existing investments, faster onboarding of new staff via a knowledge base, savings on expensive audits, savings on lawyers, fraud detection through DQ rule monitoring, accelerated decision-making, easier self-service reporting, fixing problems before they cause losses. All plausible, all unbuyable. (course day 6, slides p.19)
- Decision-speed metrics specifically are a closed topic: "attempts to measure the acceleration of decision-making were worked over some ten years ago; it seems there are no fools left" — every company that went deep on it stopped. Do not go there. (course day 6, transcript)
- A participant's objection worth carrying: there are organisations with no cost-reduction agenda at all — government bodies do not think in metrics of cutting costs — where compliance pressure runs through the management vertical rather than through money. The author concedes the skew: "honestly, yes, there's a bias in that direction," the three-source model is written for organisations that operate in money. (course day 6, transcript)

### The three methods, and what each is actually worth
- **Method 1 — the cost of a "cleaned record."** 100k identified duplicates × 100 RUB cost each, plus 100k bad records × 1,000 RUB/yr profit per newly cleaned record = 110M RUB income, against 11.5M RUB of cost (10M technical solution + 0.5M one-off cleanup + 1M/yr upkeep, mostly salaries). The cost side is spend avoided on failed communications by operators, call-centre, SMS and email; the income side is debt collection and secondary sales that depend on contact-data quality. A third term — improved attributes feeding a customer-potential model — needs a "record completeness value model" you probably don't have. Guard printed under the formula: "*one record is counted once*." (course day 6, slides p.10, 23)
- Scope condition: it only works where it is genuinely customer data, the base is large, the quality problems are real and you can assemble the figures. Otherwise "you won't pull this off." The reference case is a large bank's published MDM business case — 320M client records, 230M duplicates, initial contact-data quality 50%, acquisition cost >$300, a failed direct communication ~$300, claimed effect >4B RUB. (course day 6, transcript; slides p.11)
- **The anti-case is the one to memorise.** A company sends 10M direct-mail items a year, ~10% wrong-address or duplicate. MDM would nearly eliminate that: ~$500k/yr saved at 50 cents an item — but only $100k net after $400k/yr of new process upkeep. Against a $3M MDM investment that is **30 years' payback**. Rescuing it by bolting on marketing's advanced segmentation model gets the verdict "they probably won't buy this either." Directional lesson: *recompute the revenue side*, don't polish the savings side. (course day 6, slides p.16-18)
- **Method 2 — the catalog as labour savings.** Four role groups with separate productivity assumptions: 120 BI/data analysts/DS × 2.4M RUB × 23%; 45 report developers, app developers and enterprise architects × 2.0M × 5%; 65 data integration, governance and stewardship professionals × 1.8M × 15%; 10 regulatory compliance professionals × 2.0M × 20%. Benefit 92.29M RUB against 35M RUB cost (20M catalog TCO + 15M product team). (course day 6, slides p.20)
- And the honest label: "here a huge zone of speculation arises." The premise — that analysts spend more time working with data rather than searching, organising and preparing it — is "a huge, big assumption." It holds only if the catalog is filled, documented and decently integrated, and only pays at scale. Discount any vendor version to 5-7% provable rather than 23-26%, and remember saved time doesn't convert to output — "people just go drink coffee more often." The adjacent measurable that survives better is self-service savings, which need DG as a precondition: "there you can measure some numbers, and DG will drag it all along behind it as a locomotive." (course day 6, transcript; day 4, slides p.80-86)
- **Method 3 — business cases and freed-up profit.** A matrix of concrete business projects with income and expense per case, plus a standing DG Office cost line with zero income against it: profit growth from better targeting, purchasing/stock optimisation, theft reduction through monitoring, average-cheque growth from a recommender or from better shelf layout, headcount optimisation. Because each case must be concrete they are individually small, "and therefore you need many of them" — a dozen, at most twenty to thirty. Hunt among initiatives that already have money attached, decomposed out of the company strategy. All three methods ship ready-to-fill as sheet "11 Economics" of the DG Planner xls. (course day 6, slides p.21; transcript; day 5, slides p.36)

### Attribution, and the question that comes after the number
- In big tech, attributing value to "data governance" is simply not done — "there is no such thing there at all." Maturity is high enough that governance is split up and embedded into different processes in different places; it functions, but the benefit gets ascribed to the products it runs inside — the warehouse, the BI system, the business teams. Corroborating provocation from the room: nobody in global big tech ever stands on a stage and says "we rolled out data governance and revenue shot up." (course day 6, transcript)
- The practical consequence, from his own platform, where the exercise is valuing the ROI of the *whole data platform*: "we agree with the business that a part of the metric uplift the analyst brings will be attributed to the platform." Attribution share is a negotiated parameter, not a measurement. Fix it upfront, in writing, with the people whose metric it is. (course day 6, transcript)
- The mirror-image problem in traditional companies, from a participant: "you can come and say — well, this is needed. And you'll be told: how much money will it bring? Don't know? All right, go think again, boy." Conclusion the author does not dispute: the literal path "give us this much money for data governance" does not work; route the argument through analytics and sales effect instead. (course day 6, transcript)
- Then the counter-question most people are unprepared for: "what share of this are you prepared to book as provably saved, over a year, two, three?" Good if nobody asks. If they do, you must name the specific part of those losses you can *demonstrably* reduce — and you don't know. "You'll have to make a volitional effort and promise something — and if it doesn't work out, suffer a certain defeat in management's eyes, or in the end resign. I'm in that situation right now. Just so you don't feel alone." (course day 6, transcript)
- Which produces the escape hatch: all of this applies to companies that insist on counting every data initiative. "If you don't like that — don't go there." A funded program inherits the burden of proving payback; where money is scrutinised hard it can be better *not* to form a dedicated DG team. The one direct economy he does commit to is infrastructure: "we are now forced to set ourselves such a goal too, and to promise real savings in that block — the link there is direct: we must reduce the number of objects." (course day 6, transcript; day 5, transcript; day 2, transcript)

### When money fails, operational metrics carry the communication
- The sequencing is explicit: at the start of the road you need the money justification, "then it moves on to communication around operational DG metrics" that satisfy both your management and you — "and they themselves contain the right things inside." (course day 6, transcript)
- The scorecard ships as eight categories, each with a goal and example indicators. **Data infrastructure** — databases removed, new systems implemented with master data, % reduction in cost of owning the estate. **Data management elements** — % of critical data and processes with defined ownership, internal compliance test results, adherence to regulatory reporting deadlines, architectural and naming conformity, conformity to corporate/semantic models, metadata completeness, % of critical data in the metadata store, sources published for self-service. **Problem resolution** — % of security problems and of operational-data incidents resolved. **Organisation and people** — number of data skills, certified business data stewards, % trained employees, maturity assessment of the company and of individual units. **Data quality** — improvement per project, domain and system. **Business impact** — churn/retention and cross-sell before and after, reliability of risk models. **Time savings** — analyst time collecting and preparing data, M&A cycle time before and after, ad-hoc turnaround. **Finance** — budget to plan, ROI to plan. (course day 6, slides p.28-29)
- His own live set inside a large marketplace: % coverage by BI partners as the ownership proxy; metadata completeness and metadata age; incident resolution rate and speed; health scores per object — explicitly *not* DQ scores, "this is a bit different, it isn't data quality, it's health"; and the metric he singles out as the one business actually asks for, "days without incidents in critical reporting." (course day 6, transcript)
- Why that last one works: often nobody asks you to convert quality into money at all. "Just rid me of this pain. Enough that every day something is wrong in the reports — make it so it isn't every day, at least a few times a month." Answer the pain in the units the pain was stated in. (course day 6, transcript)
- Scorecard mechanics that make the set usable rather than decorative: collect and monitor *agreed* indicators against a baseline or YoY; every indicator has a named owner and is embedded in team and personal KPIs; regular measurement and reporting; computed either at enterprise level or at the level of a business driver; the enterprise scorecard can be decomposed into individual business-data-steward scorecards; a project-level scorecard is used when DG runs as a project. (course day 6, slides p.26-27)

### Activity metrics versus outcome metrics
- The clearest published statement of the shift is Allianz's, and he uses it as the spine of the section. Measuring *quantity* of governance: # of standards, # of business glossary terms, # of data owners, # of data stewards, # of data custodians, % of data domains with owners, # of authorised data sources, # of authorised data flows, # of policies. Measuring *business impact* of governance: # of consumer DG requirements, % of quality customer data interactions, % of processes impacted by quality, % of manual processes with automated validations, % of call-centre calls due to data defects, % of reserves held due to data issues, % of data assets changed per release. The banner: "a fundamental shift has taken place — what we may have governed 10 years ago is not what we want to govern now." (course day 6, slides p.30-31)
- Their benefit chain is the second reusable half — three goals, each wired to mechanisms and then to named business benefits: Enable Consumers (trust, reuse) → catalogs with quality measures, glossaries with lineage, data access → faster QA, faster business analysis and design, lower IT cost. Fully Automate (validation, accuracy, timeliness) → front-office, process and data-movement changes → lower business-process and call-centre cost. Provide Transparency (monitor, verify) → data operations and DQ verification → lower remediation cost and lower operational, financial and development risk. His caveat: "whether they actually calculated it, or having the chain on the slide was enough — I don't know." (course day 6, slides p.31; transcript)
- His own honest middle ground: proxy metrics — number of processes with an embedded DQ process, % of data domains meeting agreed business requirements — "in themselves don't say that any business value was achieved, but if that's sufficient for this company and this project, it may be enough." Say out loud which of your metrics are proxies. (course day 6, transcript)
- Data-product adoption is his preferred outcome proxy on the content side: count of joins and reuse parameters of certified marts — reuse is the point of the whole exercise. (course day 1, transcript)

### Scorecards that exist in the wild
- A ride-hailing big-tech's "DWH for DWH" dashboard, presented publicly and never labelled as governance: prod objects usage 37.3%, CDM/REP objects usage 13.6%, usage split by user tier and by team, per-layer usage across REP / CDM / DDS / ODS / TECH / UDM / SNB, naming conformity by domain, % of legacy objects, a matrix of unfilled descriptions by domain and attribute type. Domain data-partner metrics: share of certified objects (naming, DQ checks, descriptions), share of queries going into the certified layer, % legacy objects; attached goals include DQ incident resolution speed and the number of requests landing in support chats. "It isn't attributed to DG — but de facto it is DG." (course day 6, slides p.32; transcript)
- His own earlier scorecard at a large IT services company — a Table Metadata Completeness dashboard — is worth citing precisely because the numbers are bad: 780 tables, 21 fully described (3%), 92 partial (12%), 668 with none (86%); 24,807 columns of which 3,363 described (14%). Quality score from five weighted criteria (columns described 0.8, last update <3 months, analytics contact, DWH contact, non-empty table description ~0.045 each), aggregated by owner and by domain, per-domain scores 0.12 to 0.59, filterable so a named employee "has to green up their red patch." (course day 6, slides p.33; transcript)
- Peer evidence on what DG initiatives actually improved is thin and he says so on the slide: "assessments are rather emotional. There are almost no examples of comprehensive effectiveness and results analysis." Ride-hailing big-tech: data partners plus a catalog "gave positives," TTM effect impossible to assess "because nobody tracks Jira properly." a social platform: "entropy is decreasing." A job-search platform: deleted a lot of data, saved 20% on a 4 PB Hadoop cluster, "there hasn't been an effectiveness analysis yet — maybe we'll do one." A large marketplace: an actual analysis of search duration via support chat versus catalog search statistics, multiplied by request volume and analyst payroll — "the results gave a green light to further investment." (course day 6, slides p.34; transcript)

### Maturity models: what he actually thinks of the ladders
- The models can be treated sceptically, "as more bureaucracy" — but "I've seen many cases in practice where good maturity models really work well." The condition is precise: the company must *appropriate* one — build its own version — and embed the maturity assessment of specific domains and departments into top management's yearly goals. "That genuinely launches top-down processes." (course day 6, transcript; day 3, transcript)
- His verdict on the generic ladders is dismissive and saves you the reading: IBM 5 levels (Initial / Managed / Defined / Quantitative / Optimised), Dataflux 4 (Undisciplined / Reactive / Proactive / Governed), TDWI 6 (Prenatal → Sage), plus Gartner's EIM curve and Eckerson's. "They all look roughly like levels from zero to five with descriptions fairly similar to each other" — the progression is mostly automation of processes appearing, and ownership going none → partial → full. There is nothing to summarise; if you want one you go in and pick it yourself. (course day 6, slides p.36-38; transcript)
- Two he does recommend. **UK Government Data Maturity Framework** — best-designed open model, "neither overloaded nor superficial", 10 topics and 97 criteria across six themes (Culture 24, Data 22, Leadership 17, Tools 13, Skills 12, Uses 9), built by the UK Government Data Quality Hub on Data Orchard's product, with free self-assessment Excel sheets and five-level wording per criterion (Beginning / Emerging / Learning / Developing / Mastering). Don't limit yourself to Russian-language sources — "any LLM will translate it beautifully in five minutes." (course day 6, slides p.44-45; transcript)
- **The Central Bank of Russia's model**, published about a year ago — "visibly translated and partially compiled," but one of very few candidates to become an industry standard, because certification against Western DG models is inaccessible in Russia. (course day 6, transcript)
- Practical shortcut before any of this: hand a maturity model to an LLM, ask it to interview you with ~30 questions and produce the positioning report against the market average. "It'll most likely be a decent-quality audit result. First check what a good LLM dialogue gives you, and only then go looking by hand." (course day 6, transcript)

### Running the assessment: facilitation, not audit — and calibrating the target
- The starting problem: "as a rule, one person doesn't hold the whole picture," and often the analysis falls to someone who has just arrived. So filling any maturity model honestly is not simple, and there are three routes. **Audit**: expert on some components, twenty interviews for the rest. He did exactly this on arriving at a large marketplace — "it had limited usefulness. It let me build my roadmaps more or less." Consultants sell the same thing packaged, with sampling across roles, infrastructure audits and artifact requests. **Mailed questionnaire**: "a thankless thing" — either simple or nobody answers; he had to cut his own down drastically, "which is in principle fine too. Excess complexity gets in the way." (course day 6, transcript)
- **Facilitation**, the route he prefers: gather the fifteen or so people who matter, put the map up, stick stickers, vote on where you are, then brainstorm where to go. It packs naturally into the AS-IS stage of a data strategy session. His own map is designed for exactly this and he says so — deliberately a facilitation tool, not an assessment instrument, whose purpose was "forming a kind of road map showing what can exist at all." (course day 6, transcript; slides p.46)
- The first move on the canvas is subtractive: "as you remember, first you colour in what you *don't* need." Grey out the irrelevant bricks, colour current state, then read the development zone off the gap and vote priorities. Your maturity starts with removing what doesn't apply to you. (course day 2, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- Calibration is built in, because target maturity differs per company: four questions — headcount of decision-makers, number of power users, how data-dependent your industry is, how likely it is to be transformed by data — shift the baselines per factor. "A very simplified thing, it can't customise fully," but it stops you importing someone else's target state. The factors scored are knowing your data, getting value from data, people engagement, analytics governance, data governance, DQ management, data security management, architecture, and leadership and accountability — the last being "how much will your management actually shows." Levels run Beginner / Middle / Developing / Top; weights are editable. (course day 6, transcript; slides p.46)
- A worked reading of his own past team shows what a real profile looks like: very strong analytics governance — "a very cool analyst with governance, they managed content in the BI systems very well" — decent value extracted from data, and "quite bad with data quality management." Spiky, not a level. (course day 6, transcript)
- And maturity ≠ complexity. Decentralisation arises because a complex platform makes centralisation a bottleneck; a simpler centralised platform can be perfectly mature for a retail company. "There is no single maturity ladder" — benchmark against your archetype, not against the industry's fashion list. (course day 2, transcript)

### Benchmarks (mythology / marketing — use critically)
- Labelled "DG mythology" on the slide itself. Data Management Institute: only 30% of stored data is used regularly; storage support eats 33-70% of hardware IT spend. Gartner: 70-80% of analyst time can go to data wrangling; 15-35% of annual budget spent inefficiently due to poor DQ, up to 40% in service industries. (course day 1, slides p.43)
- DIS Group: unproductive time due to missing or low-quality data is 29-36% of working time, finance worst at 36%; market leaders run at 5-10%. The author's counter-datapoint from his own instrumentation is the useful part: his "share of analysts' target tasks" metric runs 60-70% — the loss is real but a fraction of the claimed gap. Measure your own equivalent before quoting theirs. (course day 2, slides p.44; transcript)
- Bad-data rules of thumb: data grows ~40%/yr, ~20% of a typical base is bad data, and the $1 prevent / $10 correct / $100 do-nothing pyramid per record. His annotation sits directly on the slide: "$100 per bad data record — beautiful, but unrealistic." The three-year worked example built on it ($8.495M total savings) is arithmetic on an invented constant. (course day 6, slides p.12-15)
- Eckerson Group / RateMyData: average DG maturity 2.88 of 5.0, the high end of "Initiating"; category scores Culture 3.56, Data Management 3.15, Processes 2.86, Roles 2.75, Program 2.70, Technology 2.46. Top challenges: lack of data stewards 54%, conflicting priorities 46%, lack of a plan 46%, unclear responsibilities 46%, lack of time 44%, inadequate tools 40%, resistance to change 38%, no executive support 37%. Business value delivered: high 2%, moderate 39%, low 39%, non-existent or not launched 22%. The sample behind all of it is N=59 — printed on the chart, and worth quoting whenever someone waves the 2% figure. (course day 6, slides p.39-43)
- The same instrument produced the author's own personalised report: 3.65 ("Deploying") against a peer average of 3.0, strengths in data definitions, certified reporting, DQ standards, program managers, owners/custodians and data sharing. A useful demonstration of what these self-assessments feel like from the inside — a short questionnaire producing a number precise to two decimals. (course day 6, slides p.39)
- Vendor ROI claims to copy structurally and discount numerically: Collibra's 23% analyst / 26% steward productivity; Forrester's TEI for Alation with $3.8M benefits PV against $813K costs and onboarding cut 50%. "Copy the evaluation logic, not the numbers." (course day 4, slides p.80-86; transcript)

## Maturity signals
Observable markers, assembled from his peer interviews, his own scorecards and the numbers he cites. Read them as *what you can see from outside*, not as a ladder to climb in order.

- **Stage 0 — nobody is counting and nobody minds.** Governance decisions rest on "we understand our business should work this way, full stop"; no baseline, no owner for any indicator. Legitimate as a state — most leading companies live here doing isolated sensible things and "they live and they don't break" — but you have no defence during a cost cut. (course day 6, transcript; slides p.99)
- **Stage 1 — activity counting.** The reported numbers are quantities of governance: standards, glossary terms, owners, stewards, custodians, policies, authorised sources. Nothing on the list can go down for a good reason. Companion baseline you can measure in an afternoon: 780 tables with 3% fully described, 12% partial, 86% none, 14% of columns described — a real starting picture. Ownership coverage in the same range (4-45% of assets per domain collection; ~30% at a job-search platform). Below half on either, you are Stage 1 whatever the program page says. (course day 6, slides p.30, 33; day 3, slides p.24; day 4, slides p.87)
- **Stage 2 — operational metrics with owners.** % of critical data with defined ownership, metadata completeness *and* age, incident resolution rate and speed, health scores per object kept distinct from DQ scores. The tell that it's real: each indicator has a named owner, appears in a team or personal KPI, and is reported against a baseline or YoY rather than as a level. (course day 6, slides p.27; transcript)
- **Stage 2.5 — the pain metric exists.** Somebody tracks "days without incidents in critical reporting" and the business recognises the number as an answer to what they complained about. The cheapest genuine trust signal in the whole set. (course day 6, transcript)
- **Stage 3 — content-usage metrics, published.** Share of certified objects, share of traffic or queries landing on the certified layer, % of legacy objects, usage split by user tier and team — the ride-hailing big-tech's dashboard is the reference implementation. A company can be at this stage while insisting it has no DG program. (course day 6, slides p.32)
- **Stage 3 — maturity embedded in management goals.** Domain and departmental maturity scores appear in top managers' annual goals. This is what separates a maturity model used as an instrument from one used as a slide, and it is what actually launches top-down cascades. (course day 6, transcript; day 3, transcript)
- **Stage 4 — outcome metrics tied to business processes.** % of call-centre calls caused by data defects, % of manual processes with automated validations, % of reserves held due to data issues, churn/cross-sell before and after, risk-model reliability. Rare enough that the published example everyone cites is a single insurer. (course day 6, slides p.29-31)
- **Stage 4 — attribution negotiated, not asserted.** A written agreement with business owners on what share of an analyst-driven metric uplift is credited to the data platform. Its existence matters more than the percentage. (course day 6, transcript)
- **Orthogonal signal — the infrastructure line.** A team that has committed to a real cost-of-ownership saving and can name the mechanism ("we must reduce the number of objects") is measuring something a CFO recognises; one that has deleted 20% of a 4 PB cluster has already delivered it. (course day 6, transcript; slides p.34)
- **Disqualifying signal at any stage.** The effectiveness story is "it gave positives" or "entropy is decreasing," and the reason no better number exists is that "nobody tracks Jira properly." That is the industry norm, not an excuse — but it is the difference between a program and a vibe. (course day 6, slides p.34)

## Anti-patterns
- **Metric theatre.** Reporting counts of standards, stewards and glossary terms as evidence the program works. Allianz's whole point is that this is what governance measured ten years ago and is not what anyone wants to govern now — and none of those counters can legitimately fall. (course day 6, slides p.30-31)
- **Proxy metrics presented as value.** Number of processes with an embedded DQ process, % of domains meeting agreed requirements: these "in themselves don't say that any business value was achieved." Usable — but only when you label them proxies out loud. Unlabelled, they are the polite version of metric theatre. (course day 6, transcript)
- **The castle in the clouds.** Building the case out of the indirect-benefits list — faster onboarding, savings on audits, savings on lawyers, accelerated decision-making, fraud detection, problems fixed before they cost anything. Eight items, one red X on the slide. Every one is true and none is buyable. (course day 6, slides p.19)
- **Measuring decision speed.** "Attempts to measure the acceleration of decision-making were worked over some ten years ago; there are no fools left." Every company that went deep stopped. Reinventing it burns credibility you'll need later. (course day 6, transcript)
- **ROI inflation by polishing the savings side.** The MDM anti-case: 10% error rate on 10M mailings, $500k gross saved, $100k net after new process upkeep, against $3M invested — 30 years' payback, and bolting on the marketing department's segmentation model doesn't rescue it. "You must recompute the *revenue* side." (course day 6, slides p.16-18)
- **Double-counting records.** The cleaned-record method carries a one-line guard — "one record is counted once" — because its three benefit terms (duplicate cost avoided, profit per clean record, value of improved attributes) overlap by construction. Drop the guard and the model inflates itself. (course day 6, slides p.23)
- **Speculating on the productivity percentage.** The catalog labour-savings model lives or dies on a "% productivity improvement" per role group. That field is "a huge zone of speculation"; vendors put 23-26% in it, provable reality is 5-7%, and saved time doesn't convert to output anyway — "people just go drink coffee more often." (course day 6, slides p.20; day 4, transcript)
- **Importing a benchmark as your baseline.** "29-36% of working time lost to bad data" versus his own instrumented 60-70% share of analysts' target tasks. Vendor research sets expectations you are then held to. And when you do cite it, cite the small print: "only 2% of DG programs deliver high business value" is N=59 on a vendor self-assessment site. (course day 2, slides p.44; day 6, slides p.39-43)
- **The $100-per-bad-record pyramid.** "Beautiful, but unrealistic" — and an entire three-year savings model gets built on top of it. Same family as the 1x→10,000x shift-left chart: mythology usable as a trust narrative, not as arithmetic. (course day 6, slides p.12-15; day 4, slides p.156)
- **Coverage gaming.** Two mutually gameable numbers, and you have to report both: raise checker coverage and apparent quality falls (more checkers detect more), so the cheapest way to look better is deleting checkers; but coverage itself is "easy to hack" with trivial checks, and auditing checker quality to fix that is "already overkill." Publishing only one of the pair is the anti-pattern. (course day 4, transcript; slides p.143)
- **Promising a share you can't defend.** Bringing a total to management without having decided in advance what fraction you will commit to as provably saved — and then being asked. "You'll have to make a volitional effort and promise something," with a defeat in management's eyes or a resignation on the downside. Decide the number before the meeting, not in it. (course day 6, transcript)
- **Taking the budget in a company that counts.** A funded DG program inherits the burden of proving payback. In a money-scrutinising culture, forming the dedicated team can be the worse move — governance hidden inside teams with a clearer raison d'être survives cost cuts that a DG line item does not. (course day 5, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- **Maturity model as a deck.** A five-level model adopted, presented, and never wired into anyone's annual goals. It works "if the company appropriated it"; otherwise it is bureaucracy and the sceptics in the room are right. Adjacent failure: mailing out the full-length questionnaire and waiting — "a thankless thing," it must be radically simplified or nobody answers. (course day 6, transcript)
- **Copying a target state.** Filling the maturity canvas with everything the industry has invented instead of first greying out what doesn't apply. Maturity ≠ complexity: a simpler centralised platform can be perfectly mature. (course day 2, transcript)

## Questions to ask when building the metric set
His diagnostics, roughly in the order the material works through them.

- Which of the three real ROI zones is yours — revenue growth, cost saving, or regulator risk? If the honest answer is "operational efficiency," you have air. (course day 6, slides p.4-5)
- Does your organisation even operate in money? Government bodies and some regulated structures don't think in cost reduction at all — then compliance and the management vertical are your lever, not ROI. (course day 6, transcript)
- Do you have a large enough customer base, real quality problems and access to per-record economics? If not, the cleaned-record method is unavailable to you, and you should say so before someone asks for it. (course day 6, transcript)
- What are you putting in the "% productivity improvement" cell, and who agreed to it? (course day 6, slides p.20)
- Are any of your benefit terms counting the same record, hour or incident twice? (course day 6, slides p.23)
- Who will be credited with the benefit — governance, the platform, or the business team that shipped the metric? Have you negotiated the attribution share in advance, in writing? (course day 6, transcript)
- What share of the total are you prepared to commit to as provably saved over a year? Answer it before the meeting where it is asked. (course day 6, transcript)
- Which of your metrics are outcomes, which are proxies and which are activity counts — and can you say which is which out loud? Can any headline number go *down* for a good reason? If none can, you are counting activity. (course day 6, slides p.30-31; transcript)
- Which of your numbers is gameable, and what do you publish alongside it to make gaming visible? (course day 4, slides p.143; transcript)
- Does every indicator have a named owner and appear in someone's KPI, and is it reported against a baseline or YoY rather than as a bare level? (course day 6, slides p.27)
- Has the business ever asked you for money — or did they ask you to make the pain stop? If the latter, is there a metric in *their* units, like days without incidents in critical reporting? (course day 6, transcript)
- Is there any direct economy you can commit to at all — infrastructure, objects deleted, storage freed? That is the one line that needs no attribution argument. (course day 6, transcript; day 2, transcript)
- Which bricks on the maturity map did you grey out as not applicable, and could you defend that list to your CEO? If you greyed out nothing, you haven't started. (course day 2, transcript; day 6, transcript)
- Who actually holds the picture needed to fill the model — and are you running an audit, a mailed questionnaire or a facilitation? Have you priced the difference? (course day 6, transcript)
- Are your domain or department maturity scores in anyone's annual goals? If not, what is the model for? (course day 6, transcript)
- Have you checked what a good LLM dialogue gives you — a 30-question interview and a positioning report — before doing the assessment by hand? (course day 6, transcript)
- When you quote an industry benchmark, do you know its sample size, its sponsor, and whether your own instrumentation contradicts it? (course day 2, slides p.44; day 6, slides p.39-43)
